1. What the Coupler Is For

1.1 One coil, two jobs

In the RF front-end (RFFE) block diagram, the transmit chain runs DAC → LPF → I/Q mixer → PA → BPF → coupler → coil, and the receive chain runs coil → coupler → BPF → LNA → I/Q mixer → LPF → ADC. Both chains terminate on the same coil. That is the whole design problem: the coil must radiate an interrogating field into the wrist and report back what the tissue did to it, simultaneously and on one physical port.

You cannot simply wire the PA output and the LNA input to the same node. The PA output is on the order of $+20$ dBm; the quantity you want to measure is a small change in the wave coming back from the coil. Tied together directly, the LNA sees the transmitter, not the tissue.

The one-sentence answer
The directional coupler（定向耦合器）is a four-port passive device that separates waves by direction of travel, so the receiver can be handed the wave returning from the coil while the far larger wave heading toward the coil is rejected.

1.2 Four ports, one rule

Label the ports 1–4 as in Figure 1. Two transmission lines run alongside each other over a region short enough to be treated as a single component but long enough for fields to leak between them. The device obeys one rule:

The directionality rule
Energy entering a port leaves by three paths, not four: it goes mostly straight through, a defined small fraction appears at the coupled port, and almost nothing reaches the isolated port. Reverse the direction of travel and the coupled and isolated ports swap.
Figure 1. Port convention for a backward-wave (coupled-line) directional coupler. Panel (a): the transmit wave $a$ entering P1 emerges at P2 with amplitude $\sqrt{1-c^2}\,a$ and at P3 with amplitude $c\,a$; P4 sees nothing. Panel (b): the wave $b$ returning from the coil enters P2 and now emerges at P4. The two waves are physically sorted into different ports — that is the entire function of the device. Tap P4 and you are listening only to the return.
Which port is “coupled”?
For the backward-wave coupler drawn above (the coupled-line / microstrip case), the coupled port sits at the same end as the driven port. Some lumped and waveguide structures are forward-wave couplers, where P3 and P4 swap roles. Do not memorise the picture — memorise the rule: forward and reverse waves leave by different ports, and read the datasheet to find out which is which for your part.

1.3 The four defining parameters

All four are dB numbers referred to the incident power at P1.

Coupling $$C = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_1}{P_3} = -20\log_{10}|S_{31}| \qquad\text{(how big the tap is)}$$
Isolation $$I = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_1}{P_4} = -20\log_{10}|S_{41}| \qquad\text{(leakage to the wrong port)}$$
Directivity — the figure of merit $$\boxed{\;D = 10\log_{10}\frac{P_3}{P_4} = 20\log_{10}\left|\frac{S_{31}}{S_{41}}\right| = I - C\;}$$
Insertion loss (lossless case) $$L = -20\log_{10}|S_{21}| = -10\log_{10}\!\left(1 - 10^{-C/10}\right)$$
Coupling and directivity are not the same thing
Coupling tells you how much signal you get. Directivity tells you how much you can trust it. A 20 dB coupler with 10 dB directivity is nearly useless for a reflectometer — a third of what reaches the receive port is transmitter leakage masquerading as a reflection. Directivity, not coupling, is what limits the measurement.

Typical values for a PCB or surface-mount coupler at UHF / low microwave: $C = 10$–$20$ dB, $D = 20$–$35$ dB, $L = 0.05$–$0.5$ dB.

Practice Example 1 — Power budget through the coupler

Problem: The PA delivers $+20$ dBm into a $C = 20$ dB coupler with directivity $D = 25$ dB. Assume the coupler itself is lossless.

  1. How much power reaches the coil?
  2. How much appears at the coupled port?
  3. How much transmitter power leaks into the isolated port?
Click to reveal solution

(a) The voltage coupling coefficient is $c = 10^{-C/20} = 10^{-1} = 0.1$, so a fraction $c^2 = 0.01$ of the power is tapped off:

$$L = -10\log_{10}(1-0.01) = 0.044\ \text{dB} \;\Rightarrow\; P_{\text{coil}} = 20 - 0.044 \approx +19.96\ \text{dBm}$$

Essentially all of it. A 20 dB coupler is almost invisible to the main path — this is why you can leave one permanently in-line.

(b) $P_3 = 20 - C = 20 - 20 = \mathbf{0\ dBm}$.

(c) Isolation is $I = C + D = 20 + 25 = 45$ dB, so

$$P_4 = 20 - 45 = \mathbf{-25\ dBm}$$

Sanity check（合理性驗証）: $-25$ dBm is 45 dB below the PA but still a large signal for an LNA. Remember this number — Practice 3 shows it is comparable to the entire sensing signal.

2. How Directivity Actually Arises

2.1 Two coupling mechanisms that cancel

Bring two transmission lines close together and energy leaks across by two independent mechanisms at once. This is the physical heart of the device.

  • Capacitive (electric) coupling. The mutual capacitance $C_m$ between the traces injects a displacement current into the second line. That current splits symmetrically, flowing equally toward both ends of the coupled line — in phase at P3 and P4.
  • Inductive (magnetic) coupling. The mutual inductance $L_m$ induces an EMF in the second line. A series EMF drives an antisymmetric current: equal magnitude but opposite sign at P3 and P4.
The cancellation
Design the geometry so the two contributions are equal in magnitude. Then at P3 they add, and at P4 they cancel. The cancellation at the isolated port is the entire trick, and the accuracy of that cancellation is the directivity.

The balance condition is that both mechanisms scale the same way relative to the line impedance:

Balance condition $$\frac{L_m}{L} = \frac{C_m}{C} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad Z_0 = \sqrt{Z_{0e}Z_{0o}}$$

In a homogeneous medium (stripline, coax) this holds exactly and directivity can exceed 40 dB. In an inhomogeneous medium — ordinary microstrip, where the even and odd modes see different mixtures of substrate and air — the two modes travel at different velocities, the cancellation is imperfect, and directivity typically caps out near 15–25 dB.

Practical consequence
If you need high directivity on microstrip, do not just make a longer coupled line. Use a wiggly (serrated) coupled edge to equalise the even/odd velocities, add a lumped compensation capacitor across the gap, or move to stripline / a transformer-based part.

2.2 Even/odd-mode design equations

For a quarter-wave coupled-line coupler in a $Z_0$ system with voltage coupling coefficient $c = 10^{-C/20}$, the two mode impedances follow directly:

Coupled-line synthesis $$Z_{0e} = Z_0\sqrt{\frac{1+c}{1-c}}\ ,\qquad Z_{0o} = Z_0\sqrt{\frac{1-c}{1+c}}\ ,\qquad Z_0=\sqrt{Z_{0e}Z_{0o}}$$

These two numbers are then turned into a physical trace width $w$ and gap $s$ by a field solver or by closed-form microstrip formulas. Tighter coupling means a narrower gap, which is why couplers tighter than about 6 dB are hard to fabricate as a simple edge-coupled pair.

Figure 2. The distributed realisation. Coupling is set by the gap $s$; the impedance level by the width $w$; the centre frequency by the length $\ell = \lambda_g/4$, where $\lambda_g = c_0/(f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}})$.

2.3 Frequency response

The coupled-port response of an ideal quarter-wave coupler is

Coupled port vs electrical length $$S_{31}(\theta) = \frac{j\,c\,\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-c^2}\,\cos\theta + j\sin\theta}\ ,\qquad \theta = \frac{\pi}{2}\frac{f}{f_0}$$

Coupling peaks at $\theta = 90^\circ$ ($\ell = \lambda/4$) and falls off as $\sin\theta$ on either side, giving a broad response — roughly an octave for $\pm 1$ dB flatness. That is far wider than a ferrite circulator, and it is one of the practical reasons couplers dominate in instrumentation.

Practice Example 2 — Synthesising a 20 dB coupled-line coupler

Problem: Design a 20 dB coupled-line coupler in a 50 Ω system at $f_0 = 1$ GHz on a board with $\varepsilon_{\text{eff}} = 3.0$. Find $Z_{0e}$, $Z_{0o}$, and the physical length.

Click to reveal solution

Coupling coefficient: $c = 10^{-20/20} = 0.100$.

$$Z_{0e} = 50\sqrt{\frac{1.1}{0.9}} = 50(1.1055) = \mathbf{55.3\ \Omega}$$ $$Z_{0o} = 50\sqrt{\frac{0.9}{1.1}} = 50(0.9045) = \mathbf{45.2\ \Omega}$$

Check: $\sqrt{55.3 \times 45.2} = 50.0\ \Omega$. Correct.

Length:

$$\lambda_g = \frac{c_0}{f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{\text{eff}}}} = \frac{3\times10^8}{10^9\sqrt{3}} = 173.2\ \text{mm} \;\Rightarrow\; \ell = \lambda_g/4 = \mathbf{43.3\ mm}$$

Comment: Note how close $Z_{0e}$ and $Z_{0o}$ are — only 10 Ω apart. Loose coupling means a wide gap and a nearly uncoupled pair, which is easy to fabricate but leaves directivity vulnerable to small etch and dielectric variations. Also note the length: 43 mm at 1 GHz is already awkward on a wearable board, and at 100 MHz it would be 43 cm. See §5.1.

3. Reading the RFFE Block Diagram

3.1 Signal-path walkthrough

Trace the highlighted coupler in the RFFE figure and everything falls into place:

PortConnected toCarries
P1PA → BPFTransmit drive, the interrogating tone
P2CoilNearly all the TX power out; the tissue-modulated return back in
P3Terminated (the ground symbol)A sample of the forward wave — discarded, or used as a power monitor
P4BPF → LNA → I/Q mixer → ADCThe measurement: the wave reflected by the coil
Why the ground symbols matter
The grounded port in the diagram is not a decorative detail — it is a 50 Ω termination. An unused coupler port must be terminated in the system impedance. Leave it open or shorted and the wave that should have been absorbed is re-reflected back into the coupled section, destroying directivity. A badly terminated port is the single most common cause of a reflectometer that will not calibrate.

3.2 The reflectometer — what is actually being measured

The coil presents an impedance $Z_L$ to the front end. Presenting the wrist changes the coil's near-field environment: tissue permittivity and conductivity load the coil, shifting both its inductance and its loss. The front end reads this out as a complex reflection coefficient:

The measurand $$\Gamma = \frac{b}{a} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} = |\Gamma|e^{j\phi} \;\approx\; S_{11}$$
Figure 3. The coupler as a reflectometer. The transmit wave $a$ passes through to the coil; the tissue-loaded coil returns $b = \Gamma a$; the coupler sorts $b$ into P4 and hands it to the receive chain. Because the LO comes from the same synthesiser that generated the transmit tone, the I/Q mixer recovers $\Gamma$ as a complex quantity. The unused coupler port is terminated in 50 Ω.

3.3 Why the I/Q mixer — scalar vs vector

Notice in the RFFE diagram that the splitter feeds the same signal generator (SG) tone to both the transmit and receive I/Q mixers. This is not incidental. It makes the receiver phase-coherent with the transmitter, so the baseband outputs are

Coherent detection $$I = K|\Gamma|\cos\phi\ ,\qquad Q = K|\Gamma|\sin\phi \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \Gamma = \frac{I + jQ}{K}$$
Coupler + coherent LO = vector measurement
A diode detector on the coupled port would give you only $|\Gamma|$ — a scalar reflectometer. Adding a phase-locked I/Q downconverter gives you $|\Gamma|$ and $\phi$ — a vector reflectometer, i.e. a single-port VNA on a wearable board. Phase is where most of the tissue information lives, because a change in tissue permittivity shifts the coil's reactance far more than it changes its loss.

3.4 LNA protection and wireless-charger isolation

Two secondary functions, both important in practice:

  • Receiver protection. Without the coupler's isolation, the LNA input would see the full PA output. Practice 1 showed the coupler knocks transmit leakage down to $-25$ dBm — below the $P_{1\text{dB}}$ of a typical LNA, so the receiver stays linear instead of being compressed (or destroyed) by its own transmitter.
  • Charger isolation. The greyed-out rectifier / wireless charger path shares the same coil. The coupler's directivity, together with the BPFs on both chains, keeps the strong low-frequency charging field out of the sensitive receive path. Note that this relies on frequency separation as much as on the coupler; a coupler alone does not reject an out-of-band interferer.
Practice Example 3 — Is the sensing signal above the leakage?

Problem: With $P_{\text{TX}} = +20$ dBm, a 20 dB coupler and $D = 25$ dB, the coil's reflection coefficient measures $\Gamma_{\text{off}} = 0.20\angle 30^\circ$ with no wrist present and $\Gamma_{\text{on}} = 0.26\angle 42^\circ$ with the wrist in place.

  1. What is the differential (useful) signal level at the receive port?
  2. How does it compare with the directivity leakage?
  3. What does this imply for the system design?
Click to reveal solution

(a) Work in the complex plane — this is exactly why you need a vector receiver.

$$\Gamma_{\text{on}} = 0.1932 + j0.1740\ ,\qquad \Gamma_{\text{off}} = 0.1732 + j0.1000$$ $$\Delta\Gamma = 0.0200 + j0.0740 \;\Rightarrow\; |\Delta\Gamma| = 0.0766\ ,\ \ \angle\Delta\Gamma = 74.9^\circ$$

Note that $|\Gamma|$ alone changed only from 0.20 to 0.26, but the vector change is 0.0766 — a magnitude-only receiver would have thrown away most of the phase information.

The forward wave at the coil is $\approx +20$ dBm, so the differential reflected wave is $20 + 20\log_{10}(0.0766) = 20 - 22.3 = -2.3$ dBm at the coil, and after the 20 dB coupling:

$$P_{\Delta} = -2.3 - 20 = \mathbf{-22.3\ dBm}\ \text{at P4}$$

(b) From Practice 1, transmit leakage at P4 is $-25$ dBm. The useful differential signal is therefore only

$$-22.3 - (-25) = \mathbf{2.7\ dB}$$

above the leakage. They are the same order of magnitude.

(c) Two conclusions. First, the leakage is static and coherent, so it can be removed by subtracting a stored baseline vector — the measurement is not hopeless. Second, and more importantly, any drift in that leakage (temperature, cable flex, ageing) appears directly as a false $\Delta\Gamma$. With only 2.7 dB of margin, a 1 dB drift in leakage is a large fraction of the signal. This is why such systems need periodic re-baselining, and why coupler directivity is the specification that actually limits sensitivity.

4. Directivity Sets the Sensitivity Floor

4.1 The error circle

Finite directivity means a fixed fraction of the forward wave always leaks into the receive port, where it is indistinguishable from a genuine reflection. The receiver therefore reads

Directivity error $$\Gamma_{\text{meas}} = \Gamma_{\text{true}} + \delta\ ,\qquad |\delta| = 10^{-D/20},\quad \angle\delta \ \text{unknown}$$

Because the phase of $\delta$ is unknown a priori, the true value lies somewhere on a circle of radius $|\delta|$ around the measured point:

Figure 4. The directivity error circle. The measured point can lie anywhere on a circle of radius $10^{-D/20}$ about the true reflection coefficient. Any change in $\Gamma$ smaller than this radius is not resolvable without calibration — this is the uncalibrated sensitivity floor of the whole sensor.

The resulting uncertainty on a return-loss reading follows directly:

Return-loss uncertainty $$\Delta\text{RL} = -20\log_{10}\!\left(1 \mp 10^{-(D-\text{RL})/20}\right)$$

4.2 One-port calibration removes it

The directivity error is systematic, not random — so it can be measured once and subtracted. The standard one-port error model has three terms:

One-port error model $$\Gamma_{\text{meas}} = e_{00} + \frac{e_{10}e_{01}\,\Gamma}{1 - e_{11}\Gamma}$$
  • $e_{00}$ — directivity error (the leakage term above)
  • $e_{11}$ — source match (re-reflection at the coupler port)
  • $e_{10}e_{01}$ — reflection tracking (frequency-dependent gain and phase of the path)

Three known standards — open, short, load (OSL) — give three equations for the three unknowns, and $\Gamma$ can then be solved exactly. After calibration, residual directivity is typically improved by 15–20 dB.

In a wearable, you rarely get OSL
You cannot ask a user to screw calibration standards onto a wrist strap. The practical substitute is a stored baseline: record $\Gamma_0$ with no wrist present (or at a known reference state) and report $\Delta\Gamma = \Gamma_{\text{meas}} - \Gamma_0$. This cancels $e_{00}$ and most of the tracking term at the operating point, and is why the sensing quantity in such systems is almost always a differential reading rather than an absolute impedance.

4.3 Drift is the real enemy

Once you subtract a baseline, the static leakage no longer matters. What matters is how far the leakage moves between calibration and measurement. Dominant contributors, roughly in order:

SourceMechanismMitigation
Temperature$\varepsilon_r$ and copper resistivity shift, changing even/odd velocity balanceTemperature sensor + correction, or re-baseline periodically
Cable / strap flexPhysical movement changes the reference plane and coil matchRigid mounting; keep the coupler as close to the coil as possible
Coil detuningMechanical strap tension alters coil inductance independently of tissueMechanical design; measure over frequency to separate tuning from loss
LO phase noiseDegrades the phase resolution of the I/Q readoutCommon LO for TX and RX (already done via the splitter) cancels most of it
Why the shared SG helps more than it looks
Because the transmit and receive mixers share one source through the splitter, phase noise appearing in the transmitted tone also appears in the LO, and the two correlate at the mixer output. This range correlation effect suppresses close-in phase noise substantially — the same principle that makes CW Doppler radar viable. The short electrical path from coupler to coil makes the correlation nearly perfect.
Practice Example 4 — Return-loss uncertainty from directivity

Problem: A coupler has $D = 30$ dB directivity. You measure a return loss of 15 dB on the coil. What is the uncertainty on that reading, and what would it take to halve it?

Click to reveal solution

Setup: RL $=15$ dB $\Rightarrow |\Gamma| = 10^{-15/20} = 0.1778$. The error term is $|\delta| = 10^{-30/20} = 0.0316$.

Bounds on $|\Gamma|$:

$$|\Gamma|_{\max} = 0.1778 + 0.0316 = 0.2094 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{RL} = 13.58\ \text{dB}$$ $$|\Gamma|_{\min} = 0.1778 - 0.0316 = 0.1462 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{RL} = 16.70\ \text{dB}$$

So the reading is $15\ \text{dB}\ ^{+1.70}_{-1.42}$ — roughly $\pm 1.5$ dB. Cross-checking with the closed form, with $D - \text{RL} = 15$ dB:

$$\Delta\text{RL} = -20\log_{10}(1 \mp 0.1778) = \{+1.70,\ -1.42\}\ \text{dB}\quad\checkmark$$

To halve it: the uncertainty depends on the ratio $D - \text{RL}$, so you need about 6 dB more directivity ($D \approx 36$ dB) to roughly halve the error — a hard ask from a microstrip coupler, which is exactly why calibration (§4.2) is the practical route rather than better hardware.

Note the asymmetry: the $+$ and $-$ bounds are not equal, because dB is logarithmic. Always compute in linear $\Gamma$ and convert at the end.

5. Implementation and Alternatives

5.1 Coupled-line, transformer, or bridge

Practice 2 exposed the problem: a quarter-wave coupled line is 43 mm at 1 GHz and 43 cm at 100 MHz. For a wrist-worn coil operating anywhere below roughly 1 GHz, the distributed structure is simply too big. Three realisations cover the range:

TypeFrequency rangeTypical directivityNotes
Coupled-line (microstrip/stripline)> 500 MHz15–25 dB microstrip, 35–45 dB striplineFree — it is just copper. Size set by $\lambda/4$.
Transformer / lumped1 MHz – 3 GHz20–35 dBFerrite or air-core bifilar transformer in a small SMT package. The usual choice for HF/VHF coil systems.
Resistive bridgeDC – 6 GHz35–45 dBExtremely broadband and very high directivity, but $\ge 6$ dB loss in both directions — a real noise-figure and TX-power penalty.
Which one for a wrist coil?
If the coil is resonant somewhere in the HF/VHF range, the coupler in the block diagram is almost certainly a transformer-based SMT part, not a printed coupled line — the schematic symbol (a box with an X) is drawn the same way regardless of the physical realisation. If the coil runs at low GHz, a printed coupled line becomes practical and free.

5.2 Coupler vs circulator vs T/R switch

All three can separate transmit from receive. The coupler wins here for specific reasons:

Directional couplerCirculatorT/R switch
Simultaneous TX/RXYesYesNo — time-multiplexed
BandwidthWide (octave+)Narrow (ferrite resonance)Wide
Size / cost at < 1 GHzSmall (transformer)Large, expensive, magneticSmall
TX→RX rejection$C + D$, e.g. 45 dBIsolation ~20 dBVery high when off
Loss in main path< 0.1 dB0.3–0.5 dB0.5–1.5 dB
Phase stabilityExcellent (passive, planar)Temperature-sensitive ferriteSwitching transients

For a continuous-wave reflectometer that must hold phase to a fraction of a degree, the coupler's combination of low loss, wide bandwidth, small size and thermal stability is decisive. A switch cannot transmit and receive at once, and a circulator at these frequencies would dominate the board area and drift with temperature.

5.3 Design trade-offs, summarised

Tighter coupling (e.g. 10 dB)

More signal at the receive port, better SNR at the ADC. But higher insertion loss on the main path (0.46 dB at 10 dB coupling), and more absolute transmitter leakage for the same directivity.

Looser coupling (e.g. 20–30 dB)

Nearly invisible to the transmit path and less absolute leakage, but the receive chain must supply more gain, so LNA noise figure becomes the limiting factor instead.

Higher directivity

Always good, never free. Costs board area (stripline, compensation structures) or insertion loss (resistive bridge). Beyond ~30 dB, calibration is cheaper than hardware.

Placement

Put the coupler as close to the coil as the layout allows. Every millimetre of line between coupler and coil is reference-plane error that drifts with temperature and flex.

The design rule of thumb
Choose coupling so that the expected differential reflected signal lands comfortably above the LNA noise floor, then choose directivity so that leakage drift over the operating temperature range stays below the smallest $\Delta\Gamma$ you need to resolve. In practice the second constraint binds first — which is why Practice 3's 2.7 dB margin, not the noise figure, is the number to worry about.

Three sentences, if you remember nothing else

1. The coupler exists so that one coil can transmit and be measured at the same time, by sorting waves according to which way they travel.

2. It works because capacitive and inductive coupling cancel at the isolated port; how well they cancel is the directivity.

3. Directivity — not coupling, not noise figure — sets how small a change in tissue loading the sensor can actually resolve.

  • Why does an inhomogeneous medium (microstrip) limit directivity, while stripline does not?
  • Given $C$ and $Z_0$, can you synthesise $Z_{0e}$ and $Z_{0o}$ and check them with $Z_0 = \sqrt{Z_{0e}Z_{0o}}$?
