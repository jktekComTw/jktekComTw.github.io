The difference between the “three kinds of circles” on the Smith chart

It depends which “circle” you mean — the Smith chart carriesthree different kinds of circle, and they are frequently conflated. Below, their centres, physical meanings and design uses are set out clearly.

Circle 1: the constant-$|\Gamma|$ circle (concentric circles)

  • The centre is always at thecentre
  • of the Smith chart; radius = $|\Gamma|$
  • Physical meaning: every point on the circle hasthe same VSWR / return loss
  • Use: the locus traced out by rotation along a series transmission line (which changes the phase but not $|\Gamma|$)
  • Nothing to do with gain
▸ How do you obtain this circle? (click to expand)

It needs only one input: an impedance (or a reflection coefficient). The procedure is:

Three steps

Step 1 — compute the reflection coefficient from the impedance $Z$

Given any impedance $Z$ and a reference impedance $Z_0 = 50\,\Omega$:

$$\Gamma = \frac{Z - Z_0}{Z + Z_0}$$

Step 2 — take the magnitude

$$|\Gamma| = \left|\frac{Z - Z_0}{Z + Z_0}\right|$$

This value is the circle'sRadius(in the range $0 \le |\Gamma| \le 1$).

Step 3 — draw the circle

Draw a circle of radius $|\Gamma|$ centred on the middle of the Smith chart (the point $\Gamma = 0$, corresponding to $Z_0 = 50\,\Omega$).

A worked example

Take $Z = 100 + j50\,\Omega$:

$$\Gamma = \frac{(100+j50) - 50}{(100+j50) + 50} = \frac{50+j50}{150+j50} \approx 0.4 + j0.2 = 0.447\angle 26.6°$$ $$\Rightarrow\;\; |\Gamma| = 0.447$$

→ draw a circle of radius $0.447$ about the origin, and that is the constant-$|\Gamma|$ circle corresponding to $Z = 100+j50$.

Physical meaning — why a “circle” and not some other shape

If you connect a length oflossless 50 Ω transmission lineto the load $Z$, then as you move along the line the impedance you see is different at every point, but themagnitude of the reflection coefficient does not change— only itsphase changesat some port:

$$\Gamma(\ell) = \Gamma_L \cdot e^{-j2\beta\ell}$$
  • $|\Gamma(\ell)| = |\Gamma_L|$ ← the magnitude is unchanged
  • $\angle\Gamma(\ell) = \angle\Gamma_L - 2\beta\ell$ ← the phase changes with distance

In the complex plane, the locus of “constant magnitude, varying phase” is preciselya circle centred on the origin — that is the physical origin of the constant-$|\Gamma|$ circle.

▸ Why $2\beta\ell$ and not $\beta\ell$? (click to expand)

The key point: $\Gamma$ is the ratio “reflected wave / incident wave” — its phase accumulates overboth legs of the round tripalong the line.

Derivation

On a lossless line the voltage is the superposition of the forward wave (towards the load) and the reflected wave (towards the source):

$$V^+(z) = V_0^+ e^{-j\beta z}, \qquad V^-(z) = V_0^- e^{+j\beta z}$$

Thelocal reflection coefficientat some port:

$$\Gamma(z) = \frac{V^-(z)}{V^+(z)} = \frac{V_0^- e^{+j\beta z}}{V_0^+ e^{-j\beta z}} = \underbrace{\frac{V_0^-}{V_0^+}}_{=\,\Gamma_L\text{ at }z=0} \cdot e^{+j2\beta z}$$

at position $z$. Placing the load at $z = 0$ and moving a distance $\ell$ towards the source (i.e. $z = -\ell$):

$$\boxed{\;\Gamma(\ell) = \Gamma_L \cdot e^{-j2\beta\ell}\;}$$

Physical intuition: two legs, out and back

  • The wave travels from the input to the load: it accumulates a phase $\beta\ell$ (the first leg)
  • After reflecting, it travels from the load back to the input: another $\beta\ell$ (the second leg)
  • In total: the phase of $\Gamma$ changes by $= 2\beta\ell$ (one round trip)

By contrast, the impedance $Z(\ell)$ is the instantaneous ratio “voltage / current”, not a round-trip quantity, which is why its formula contains $\tan(\beta\ell)$ and not $\tan(2\beta\ell)$.

Converting to units of $\lambda$

$\beta = 2\pi/\lambda$, so:

$$2\beta\ell = 2 \cdot \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \cdot \ell = 4\pi \cdot \frac{\ell}{\lambda} = 4\pi L$$

where $L = \ell/\lambda$ is the slider value. In the code this becomes:

const ang1 = ang0 - 4*Math.PI*L; // 2βℓ = 4π·L

Key values of $\ell$

$\ell$$2\beta\ell$Rotation on the circle
$\lambda/8$$90°$a quarter turn
$\lambda/4$$180°$half a turn (impedance inversion, an inverter)
$\lambda/2$$360°$a full turn back to the start — which is the slider's upper limit

The full turn every $\lambda/2$ is exactly the “$\lambda/2$ periodicity of a transmission line” — adding or subtracting $\lambda/2$ from the same line length leaves the impedance and reflection coefficient completely unchanged.

So why is the stub formula $\tan(\beta\ell_s)$ and not $\tan(2\beta\ell_s)$?

This isthe same physical fact expressed in two different domainsat some port:

ExpressionAngleReason
$\Gamma$ rotation (reflection-coefficient domain)$2\beta\ell$$\Gamma = V^-/V^+$ is the ratio of two counter-propagating waves; move by $\ell$ and their phases differ by $2\beta\ell$
$\tan(\beta\ell)$ (impedance / admittance domain)$\beta\ell$$Z = V/I$ is the superposition of the two waves atthe same point, and on simplification the factor of 2 is absorbed by a trigonometric identity

Verification: work it through once with a short-circuit stub. For a short circuit $\Gamma_L = -1$; moving $\ell_s$ towards the source:

$$\Gamma_{\text{in}} = -e^{-j2\beta\ell_s}$$

Converting $\Gamma_{\text{in}}$ back to an impedance:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0\,\frac{1+\Gamma_{\text{in}}}{1-\Gamma_{\text{in}}} = Z_0\,\frac{1-e^{-j2\beta\ell_s}}{1+e^{-j2\beta\ell_s}}$$

Using the trigonometric identity $\dfrac{1-e^{-j2x}}{1+e^{-j2x}} = j\tan x$:

$$\boxed{\;Z_{\text{in}} = jZ_0\tan(\beta\ell_s)\;}$$

The $2\beta\ell_s$ originally in the exponent is absorbed into $\tan(\beta\ell_s)$ during the simplification — the factor of 2 has not disappeared, it is merely “hidden” inside the trigonometric identity.

A mnemonic

Which quantityWhich angle
Rotating around a circle on the Smith chart ($\Gamma$ domain)$2\beta\ell$(round trip)
Computing a stub's $b$ or $x$ ($Y$ / $Z$ domain)$\beta\ell$(one way)

Whenever you see a $\tan$ or $\cot$ formula it isone way $\beta\ell$; whenever you see an $e^{-j(\cdot)}$ rotation it isround trip $2\beta\ell$.

In one sentence

$\Gamma$ is a round-trip quantity, so its rotation angle is $2\beta\ell$. A full turn for “toward generator $\lambda/2$” on the Smith chart is a direct consequence of that factor of 2. The stub's $\tan(\beta\ell_s)$ actually contains the same factor of 2; it has simply been absorbed by a trigonometric identity so that it looks like a one-way trip.

Circle 2: the constant-$G_S$ circle (gain circle)

  • The centre isnot at the middle; it lies on the ray joining $O$ to $S_{11}^*$
  • It is fixed jointly by $S_{11}$ and the target $G_S$ (the formula is rather involved)
  • Physical meaning: every point on the circle producesthe same source gain $G_S$
  • Use: it gives youseveral equivalent candidate positions for $\Gamma_s$to choose from

What do the points on the $G_S$ circle represent?

The statement “the impedance values represented by points on the $G_S$ circle” is correct — because:

Position on the $G_S$ circle$\Gamma_s$ valueCorresponding $z_s$
The centre point (maximum gain)$S_{11}^*$$z_s = (1+S_{11}^*)/(1-S_{11}^*)$
Other points on the circleA different $\Gamma_s$A different $z_s$

All of these points have different $\Gamma_s$ / $z_s$, yet they all give the same $G_S$. The designer can pick whichever impedance on the circle is most convenient to realise (avoiding points too close to a short or open, or points outside the stable region).

Circle 3: the constant-$g$ circle (constant-conductance circle)

  • The centre is at $(-0.5,\,0)$ with radius $0.5$ (for the $g=1$ circle; other values of $g$ are similar)
  • Physical meaning: every point on the circle hasthe same real part $g$ of the normalized admittance
  • Use: the locus of a shunt stub — adding $jb$ slides you along a constant-$g$ circle

Derivation

An open-circuit stub is terminated in $Z_L \to \infty$ (an open circuit). Substituting into the transmission-line input-impedance formula and taking the limit:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0 \cdot \frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan(\beta\ell_s)}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan(\beta\ell_s)} \xrightarrow{Z_L\to\infty} \frac{Z_0}{j\tan(\beta\ell_s)} = -j Z_0 \cot(\beta\ell_s)$$
▸ Where does the transmission-line input-impedance formula come from? (click to expand)

Step 1 — the general solution for voltage and current on the line

The voltage and current on a lossless transmission line are the superposition of aforward wave + and a reflected wave:

$$V(z) = V^+ e^{-j\beta z} + V^- e^{+j\beta z}$$ $$I(z) = \frac{V^+}{Z_0} e^{-j\beta z} - \frac{V^-}{Z_0} e^{+j\beta z}$$

Coordinate system: placethe load at $z = 0$andthe input at $z = -\ell$of the image-reject mixer at low IF.

Step 2 — apply the boundary condition at the load

At $z = 0$:

$$V(0) = V^+ + V^-, \qquad I(0) = \frac{V^+ - V^-}{Z_0}$$

The load impedance $Z_L = V(0)/I(0)$:

$$Z_L = Z_0 \cdot \frac{V^+ + V^-}{V^+ - V^-}$$

Dividing $V(0)$ by $I(0)$ directly gives this. The numerator is theand from theof the two wave voltages, and the denominator is theirPoor — because the reflected wave travels in the $-z$ direction, the relation $I = V/Z_0$ picks up a minus sign for it, so $V^-$ enters $I(0)$ with a negative sign.

Definitionload reflection coefficientat some port:

$$\Gamma_L = \frac{V^-}{V^+} = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$$

Dividing both numerator and denominator of $Z_L$ above by $V^+$ rewrites it inreflection-coefficient form(equivalent to the expression above):

$$Z_L = Z_0 \cdot \frac{1 + \Gamma_L}{1 - \Gamma_L}$$

Step 3 — compute the input impedance

At $z = -\ell$:

$$V(-\ell) = V^+\!\left[e^{+j\beta\ell} + \Gamma_L e^{-j\beta\ell}\right]$$ $$I(-\ell) = \frac{V^+}{Z_0}\!\left[e^{+j\beta\ell} - \Gamma_L e^{-j\beta\ell}\right]$$

Dividing the two:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0 \cdot \frac{e^{+j\beta\ell} + \Gamma_L e^{-j\beta\ell}}{e^{+j\beta\ell} - \Gamma_L e^{-j\beta\ell}}$$

Step 4 — simplify (substitute $\Gamma_L$ and use Euler's formula)

Substituting $\Gamma_L = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$ and multiplying numerator and denominator by $(Z_L + Z_0)$:

Numerator:

$$(Z_L + Z_0)e^{+j\beta\ell} + (Z_L - Z_0) e^{-j\beta\ell} = 2Z_L \cos(\beta\ell) + 2j Z_0 \sin(\beta\ell)$$

Denominator (by analogy):

$$2Z_0 \cos(\beta\ell) + 2j Z_L \sin(\beta\ell)$$

Substituting back and then dividing numerator and denominator by $\cos(\beta\ell)$:

$$\boxed{\,Z_{\text{in}} = Z_0 \cdot \frac{Z_L + j Z_0 \tan(\beta\ell)}{Z_0 + j Z_L \tan(\beta\ell)}\,}$$

The core physics

There are only two facts behind the whole formula:

  1. the wave on the line is the superposition of a forward and a reflected wave(the lossless-line assumption)
  2. the load determines the reflection coefficient $\Gamma_L$

Tidy those up with a trigonometric identity and you have one of the most important formulas in transmission-line theory.

How to convert $\beta$ and $\ell_s$ into units of $\lambda$

The phase constant is $\beta = 2\pi/\lambda$, so:

$$\beta\ell_s = 2\pi \cdot \frac{\ell_s}{\lambda}$$

For example with $\ell_s = 0.100\lambda$: $\beta\ell_s = 36°$ and $b = \tan(36°) \approx 0.727$.

Why “open” rather than “short”

  • Easier to fabricate on a PCB: open = the line simply stops there; short = a via to ground is needed, which adds a process step and parasitic inductance.
  • It covers the whole susceptance range: an open stub from $\ell_s = 0$ to $\lambda/4$ provides $b \in (0,+\infty)$, and from there to $\lambda/2$ it provides $b \in (-\infty, 0)$, covering the entire susceptance range.
  • The corresponding short-circuit stub differs only by $\lambda/4$ in length: $b_{\text{short}} = -\cot(\beta\ell_s)$, which is the open-stub curve translated by $\lambda/4$.

▸ Why $g=1$? (click to expand)

It is not that $g=1$ is the only option — it is because the demo'sstarting point is the centre of the Smith chart($y = 1$, $g = 1$). A shunt stub changes only $b$ and not $g$, so starting from the centre you must slide along the $g=1$ circle.

The physical rule

Adding a pure susceptance $jb_{\text{stub}}$ in shunt:

$$y_{\text{new}} = y_{\text{old}} + jb_{\text{stub}}$$

The real part is unchanged ($g$ is conserved) and only the imaginary part changes:

$$g_{\text{new}} = g_{\text{old}}, \qquad b_{\text{new}} = b_{\text{old}} + b_{\text{stub}}$$

→ The point slides along a “constant-$g$ circle”, and the value of $g$ is set by thestarting point.

Figure 1 has nothing to do with the impedance / admittance viewpoint

The existence of the constant-$|\Gamma|$ circle (figure 1)does not depend on distinguishing an impedance from an admittance viewpoint — it is simply the concentric circle at a fixed distance from the centre of the Smith chart, defined purely by the magnitude of $\Gamma$.

Whether you read that point as $z = 0.6 + j0.4$ (impedance) or $y = 1.143 - j0.742$ (admittance), it lies onthe very same $\|\Gamma\|$ circle.

Conclusion:
  • Figure 1 vs figure 3: entirely different circles, not two viewpoints on impedance / admittance.
  • Figure 3 vs the constant-$r=1$ circle: *that* is the “one structure, two viewpoints” relationship (the $\Gamma_y = -\Gamma_z$ mirror).

Why does “radius = 0” mean maximum available gain?

▸ Maximum available power gain occurs when the radius of the constant input (or output) power gain circle is zero — why?

Geometric intuition

A constant-$G_P$ (or $G_A$) circle isthe set of all $\Gamma_L$ producing the same gain value. Each point on the circle is a different load impedance, yet they all give the same power gain.

Turn $G_P$ up from small values and watch what happens to the circle:

Value of $G_P$Circle radiusMeaning
smalllargePlenty of $\Gamma_L$ options — this gain is easy to achieve.
largersmallerFewer $\Gamma_L$ options — the design space narrows.
$G_{P,\max}$0 (a single point)Only one$\Gamma_L$ can still reach this gain — the simultaneous-conjugate-match $\Gamma_{ML}$.
beyond $G_{P,\max}$imaginary / negativeNo real $\Gamma_L$ can produce a gain that large — physically impossible.

So “radius = 0” marksthe boundary between achievable and unachievable. Just below it there is a very small circle (lots of options crowded around $\Gamma_{ML}$); exactly at it there is a single point; beyond it the circle does not exist.

The mathematical derivation

The radius formula for the operating-gain circles (in the $\Gamma_L$ plane):

$$R_P \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{1 - 2K|S_{12}S_{21}|\,g_P + |S_{12}S_{21}|^2 g_P^2}}{\bigl|\,1 + g_P(|S_{22}|^2 - |\Delta|^2)\,\bigr|}, \qquad g_P = \frac{G_P}{|S_{21}|^2}$$

Set $R_P = 0$ — that is, make the discriminant under the square root vanish:

$$|S_{12}S_{21}|^2 g_P^2 \;-\; 2K|S_{12}S_{21}|\,g_P \;+\; 1 \;=\; 0$$

Solving this quadratic in $g_P$:

$$g_P \;=\; \frac{K \pm \sqrt{K^2 - 1}}{|S_{12}S_{21}|}$$

Taking the smaller root (the larger root corresponds to the unphysical “negative radius” region) and multiplying by $|S_{21}|^2$:

$$\boxed{\;G_{P,\max} \;=\; \frac{|S_{21}|}{|S_{12}|}\Bigl(K - \sqrt{K^2 - 1}\Bigr)\;}$$

This is exactly the maximum available gain($G_{MAG}$). It exists only when $K > 1$ (unconditionally stable); otherwise $\sqrt{K^2-1}$ is imaginary — which is also why MAG is undefined for a conditionally stable device.

The intuition in one sentence

Each gain circle is the “$\Gamma_L$ menu” for one gain value. The higher the gain you want, the shorter the menu. At maximum gain there is only one dish left on it — the conjugate-match point $\Gamma_{ML}$. Ask for any more and the menu is empty.

Why do the input and output circles shrink to points at the same time?

At maximum available gain the device is in a state of simultaneous conjugate match: $\Gamma_S = \Gamma_{MS}$ and $\Gamma_L = \Gamma_{ML}$, satisfying $\Gamma_{in} = \Gamma_S^*$ and $\Gamma_{out} = \Gamma_L^*$ simultaneously. Because $S_{12}$ couples the input and output ends together, you cannot shrink the output circle to a point while leaving the input circle finite — they reach zero radius at thesamegain value together. That is why the slide says “input or output”.

▸ What is MAG? (Maximum Available Gain)

Definition

MAG (Maximum Available Gain) = the maximum power gain a two-port device (usually a transistor) can achieve undersimultaneous conjugate match .

Formula:

$$G_{MAG} = \frac{|S_{21}|}{|S_{12}|}\Bigl(K - \sqrt{K^2 - 1}\Bigr) \qquad (\text{defined only for } K > 1)$$

What the word “available” means

The definition of available gain $G_A$:

$$G_A = \frac{\text{power available from the device to the load}}{\text{power available from the source to the device}}$$

Note that “available” presupposesthat the downstream end is already perfectly conjugate-matchedand extracts all of the available power. Hence $G_A$ depends only on $\Gamma_S$and not on the actual $\Gamma_L$ (since the output is assumed to take all of the available power).

When you also choose $\Gamma_S$ to maximise $G_A$ — that is, $\Gamma_S = \Gamma_{MS}$ — both ends are conjugate-matched at once, and that maximum is MAGof the image-reject mixer at low IF.

The three gains in one table

NameSymbolAssumptionFormula (after optimization)
Transducer gain$G_T$Actual input + actual output (both ends may be mismatched)Depends on $\Gamma_S, \Gamma_L$
Available gain$G_A$Actual $\Gamma_S$,output conjugate-matched$\dfrac{|S_{21}|^2 (1-|\Gamma_S|^2)}{|1-S_{11}\Gamma_S|^2 (1-|\Gamma_{out}|^2)}$

The relationship between the three: $G_T \le G_A \le G_{MAG}$, with equality when the corresponding matching condition is met.

Why it matters in practice

  • MAG is the device's “gain ceiling”: at this frequency, no matter how you design the matching networks, the most passive matching can extract from this transistor is MAG.
  • A number telling you whether the device is any good: look at the MAG-versus-frequency curve on a datasheet and you know how high in frequency the device still has useful gain.
  • At $K = 1$ it degenerates into MSG: $G_{MAG} \to \dfrac{|S_{21}|}{|S_{12}|}$, the gain ceiling on the stability boundary, and the target for a conditionally stable device once a stabilizing circuit has been added.
In one sentence: MAG = the maximum power gain a device can be squeezed for under the ideal condition of “simultaneous conjugate match at both ends”. It is a property of the device itself (determined by the S-parameters plus K) and has nothing to do with the $\Gamma_S, \Gamma_L$ you choose — because it already presumes you chose the optimal pair that conjugate-matches both ends at once.
▸ Why is MAG undefined for a conditionally stable device ($K<1$)?

The mathematical breakdown

The MAG formula:

$$G_{MAG} = \frac{|S_{21}|}{|S_{12}|}\Bigl(K - \sqrt{K^2 - 1}\Bigr)$$

When $K < 1$ (conditionally stable):

  • $K^2 - 1 < 0$
  • $\sqrt{K^2-1}$ becomespurely imaginary
  • and the whole of $G_{MAG}$ becomes complex — there is no real number to call the “maximum available gain”

The physical meaning — why this happens

Return to the picture of “the circle shrinking to a point”. When $K > 1$, turning $G_P$ up makes the circle shrink monotonicallyfrom the outside inwards, finally collapsing onto the single point $\Gamma_{ML}$, at which MAG is reached.

When $K < 1$, the circledoes not shrink monotonically. It does one of two “bad” things:

BehaviourConsequence
The circle grows larger and larger, evenenclosing or crossing the unit-circle boundary of the Smith chartThe “corresponding optimal $\Gamma_L$” ends up in the region $|\Gamma_L| \ge 1$ — not a passive load, but one that would have to supply power actively
The circle itself stays inside the unit circle, but $\Gamma_{out}$ or $\Gamma_{in}$ moves outside itThe device becomes an oscillator, not an amplifier

In other words: the simultaneous conjugate match $\Gamma_{MS}, \Gamma_{ML}$ can still be solved for “formally”, butthat pair of solutions falls in the unstable regionand is not a physically realizable passive match.

In one sentence

$K > 1$: the gain ceiling is cleanly defined by “the circle shrinking to a point” → that point is MAG.
$K < 1$: the gain ceiling is cut short by stability → the gain you can obtain is no longer limited by the shrinking circle but by “not letting the device oscillate” → MAG loses its meaning.

The substitute — MSG (Maximum Stable Gain)

For a conditionally stable device, engineers use instead:

$$G_{MSG} = \frac{|S_{21}|}{|S_{12}|}$$

which is precisely the value of the MAG formula with $K = 1$ ($\sqrt{K^2-1} = 0$). Its meaning:if you can stabilize the device onto the $K = 1$ boundary with an external resistor, this is the most gain you can get. Any more and you must sacrifice stability.

So the practical design procedure for a conditionally stable device is:

  1. First add a stabilizing resistor or feedback to pull $K$ above 1
  2. Then compute MAG for the stabilized device
  3. Or simply accept MSG as the design ceiling
▸ Why is $g_A = G_A / |S_{21}|^2 > 1$ typically?

First, the physical meaning of $|S_{21}|^2$

$|S_{21}|^2$ is the value of the transducer gain $G_T$ whenboth ends are terminated directly in $Z_0$($\Gamma_S = 0,\;\Gamma_L = 0$):

$$G_T \Big|_{\Gamma_S=0,\;\Gamma_L=0} = |S_{21}|^2$$

That is, the most rudimentary case of “no matching at all”: the source connected straight to the device through 50 Ω, and the load likewise 50 Ω.

Why available gain must be larger than that

$G_A$ is defined with the actual $\Gamma_S$, but withthe output already conjugate-matched(the load end takes all of the available power). So even if youhave not matched the input yet($\Gamma_S = 0$), $G_A$ already includes an “output-matching bonus”:

$$G_A \Big|_{\Gamma_S = 0} = \frac{|S_{21}|^2}{1 - |S_{22}|^2}$$

because when $\Gamma_S = 0$ we have $\Gamma_{out} = S_{22}$, and conjugate-matching the outputgives backthe factor $(1-|S_{22}|^2)$ that was lost with $\Gamma_L = 0$. Hence:

$$g_A \Big|_{\Gamma_S = 0} = \frac{G_A}{|S_{21}|^2} = \frac{1}{1 - |S_{22}|^2} \;>\; 1$$

which already guarantees $g_A > 1$ — before any input matching at all.

A concrete example

Take a typical RF transistor: $|S_{11}| = 0.6,\; |S_{22}| = 0.5,\; |S_{21}| = 2.5,\; |S_{12}| = 0.1$

Choice of $\Gamma_S$Approximate $g_A$
$\Gamma_S = 0$ (no input matching)$1/(1-0.5^2) = 1.33$
Somewhere on an intermediate gain circle$\sim 2$ – $3$
$\Gamma_S = \Gamma_{MS}$ (max available)possibly $5$ – $10$

Each constant-$G_A$ circle corresponds to one value of $g_A$; the circle you want to draw is the one that makes $G_A$ reach some target $G_{Ak}$, so $g_A = G_{Ak}/|S_{21}|^2$ is naturally $> 1$.

Why this normalization is useful

Using $G_A$ (a linear ratio) directly mixes it up with the device's own characteristics; dividing by $|S_{21}|^2$ gives $g_A$, the dimensionless quantity “how many times better than the zero-matching baseline”.

The centre and radius formulas (the bilateral case):

$$C_A = \frac{g_A\,C_1^*}{1 + g_A(|S_{11}|^2 - |\Delta|^2)}, \qquad R_A = \frac{\sqrt{1 - 2K|S_{12}S_{21}|g_A + |S_{12}S_{21}|^2 g_A^2}}{\bigl|1 + g_A(|S_{11}|^2 - |\Delta|^2)\bigr|}$$

Notice that all the formulas take $g_A$ directly rather than $G_A$ — once $|S_{21}|^2$ is factored out, what remainsdepends only on the matching state, and the formulas take a clean form.

In one sentence: $|S_{21}|^2$ = the “bare gain” baseline with no matching whatsoever. $g_A$ = the factor by which your target gain exceeds that baseline. As long as your target isbetter than plugging it straight into 50 Ω(which is virtually always the case, since conjugate-matching the output alone squeezes out a factor of $1/(1-|S_{22}|^2)$), $g_A$ is $> 1$.

The exception (when $g_A < 1$ does happen)

Only if youdeliberatelydeliberately choose a target gain worse than $|S_{21}|^2$ (sacrificing gain for extremely wide bandwidth or an excellent noise match, say) does $g_A$ fall below 1. Such designs turn up occasionally in LNAs but rarely in general amplifier design — which is why the slide says “typically” rather than “always”.

The meaning of the four reflection coefficients $\Gamma_s,\,\Gamma_l,\,\Gamma_{in},\,\Gamma_{out}$ and how they differ
Symbol Direction Set by Physical meaning
$\Gamma_s$ Looking towards the source (outside port 1) Chosen by the designer(the input matching network) The reflection coefficient of the source impedance against $Z_0$; you use it to pick an operating point on a gain circle or a noise circle
$\Gamma_l$ Looking towards the load (outside port 2) Chosen by the designer(the output matching network) The reflection coefficient of the load impedance against $Z_0$; usually set to $\Gamma_{out}^*$ to achieve a conjugate match
$\Gamma_{in}$ Looking into the transistor from port 1 Set by the device together with $\Gamma_l$ The equivalent reflection coefficient at the transistor input; when $S_{12}\neq 0$ it depends on $\Gamma_l$: $\Gamma_{in} = S_{11} + \dfrac{S_{12}S_{21}\Gamma_l}{1-S_{22}\Gamma_l}$
$\Gamma_{out}$ Looking into the transistor from port 2 Set by the device together with $\Gamma_s$ The equivalent reflection coefficient at the transistor output; when $S_{12}\neq 0$ it depends on $\Gamma_s$: $\Gamma_{out} = S_{22} + \dfrac{S_{12}S_{21}\Gamma_s}{1-S_{11}\Gamma_s}$

A way to remember it

  • $\Gamma_s$ and $\Gamma_l$ areexternal, under the designer's control — the impedances youpresent tothe transistor.
  • $\Gamma_{in}$ and $\Gamma_{out}$ areinternal, the reflections presented by the transistor itself (together with the load on the other side) — the impedances the transistorpresents tothe external circuit.
