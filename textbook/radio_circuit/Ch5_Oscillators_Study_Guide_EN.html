5.1 Oscillator Models & Circuits

5.1.1 Overview & Key Specifications

What an Oscillator Is
An oscillator converts DC power into a steady-state RF sinusoid using two ingredients:
  • Active component (transistor or negative-resistance diode) — supplies the power that sustains oscillation.
  • Passive resonator (LC tank, crystal, cavity, dielectric resonator, transmission line) — selects the frequency.

Frequency ranges

  • Transistor oscillators — up to several GHz, often with crystal resonators or PLLs for stability.
  • Negative-resistance diodes (IMPATT, Gunn, BARITT) — used at higher frequencies with cavity, transmission-line, or dielectric resonators, up to 100 GHz.

Four key specifications

5.1.2 RF Oscillator Model — Amplifier with Feedback Network

An amplifier with voltage gain $A$ produces output $V_o$. This output passes through a feedback network with frequency-dependent transfer function $H(\omega)$, and is added to the input $V_i$:

Closed-loop output $$V_o(\omega) = A\bigl[V_i(\omega) + H(\omega)V_o(\omega)\bigr] \;\Rightarrow\; V_o(\omega) = \frac{A}{1-AH(\omega)}V_i(\omega)$$
Barkhausen / Nyquist Criterion
At the oscillation frequency $\omega_0$, the loop gain satisfies $$AH(\omega_0)=1 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \begin{cases}|AH(\omega_0)|=1 & \text{(magnitude condition)}\\ \angle AH(\omega_0)=0\text{ (mod }360°\text{)} & \text{(phase condition)}\end{cases}$$ Then a finite $V_o$ exists even with $V_i = 0$ — the loop sustains itself.

1. Definition of Phase Margin

Phase margin (PM) is the key metric for thestabilityof a control system or feedback amplifier.

Mathematical definition

For the open-loop transfer function $ L(j\omega) = G(j\omega)H(j\omega) $:

$$ PM = 180° + \angle L(j\omega_{gc}) $$

where $ \omega_{gc} $ is thegain crossover frequency, the frequency at which $ |L(j\omega_{gc})| = 1 $ (i.e. 0 dB).

Reading off stability

Phase MarginSystem behaviour
PM > 0°Stable
PM < 0°Unstable
PM ≈ 45°–60°Well-behaved transient response (the usual industry design target)
PM < 30°Heavily oscillatory response, pronounced ringing
PM → 0°Close to the oscillation boundary

2. The Barkhausen Oscillation Criterion

When designing an oscillator you must simultaneously satisfytwo conditions:

ConditionExpressionPhysical meaning
Magnitude condition $ |AH(\omega_0)| = 1 $ The loop gain magnitude is 1 — the signal is not attenuated on one trip round the loop
Phase condition $ \angle AH(\omega_0) = 0° \pmod{360°} $ The signal comes back in phase after one trip round the loop, so positive feedback holds

where:

  • $ A $: amplifier gain
  • $ H $: feedback-network transfer function
  • $ AH $:loop gain
  • $ \omega_0 $: oscillation frequency
Designing an oscillator meansdeliberatelyforcing $ \angle AH = 0° $ (equivalently a phase of −180° under negative feedback) with $ |AH| \geq 1 $ → deliberately making PM = 0so that the circuit oscillates.

3. LTI vs. Nonlinear: Two Kinds of Stability

The LTI view: an oscillator is “unstable”

From small-signal LTI analysis (Bode / Nyquist / pole location):

  • Loop gain $ |AH(\omega_0)| \geq 1 $
  • Phase $ \angle AH(\omega_0) = 0° $
  • the poles sit on the $ j\omega $ axis or even in the right half-plane (RHP)
  • PM ≤ 0, GM ≤ 0, so the system is “unstable”

This is also why, at start-up, thermal noise or the power-on transient is amplified exponentially → oscillation builds up.

The nonlinear view: a stable limit cycle exists

But a real circuitdoes not blow up, because:

  1. The transistor enters saturation / cut-off / triode → the transconductance $ g_m $ falls as the amplitude grows
  2. The effective loop gain isamplitude-dependent: $ |AH| = f(A) $
  3. The system converges to astable limit cycle

The two kinds of stability side by side

ViewpointAnalysis toolsEquilibriumVerdict on the oscillator
LTI / local linearization Bode, Nyquist, Routh, poles The origin $ x = 0 $ Unstable (poles in the RHP or on the jω axis)
Nonlinear / global Lyapunov, Poincaré-Bendixson, describing function The limit cycle $ \Gamma $ Stable (the limit cycle is orbitally stable)
The key difference:
  • LTI asks “is the origin stable?” → unstable
  • The nonlinear view asks “is the limit cycle stable?” → stable
The two arenot contradictory; they describe different features of the very same system.

4. Van der Pol Equation

$$ \ddot{x} - \mu(1 - x^2)\dot{x} + x = 0 $$

Everything hinges on the damping term $ -\mu(1 - x^2) $:

The phase-plane picture

Phase plane of the Van der Pol system: the origin is an unstable spiral (red), Γ is a stable limit cycle (green), and every non-zero initial state converges to Γ (blue)
  • The origin is an unstable spiral(what LTI sees)
  • The limit cycle is a stable orbit(what the nonlinear view sees)
  • Anynon-zeroinitial condition eventually converges to the limit cycle → this is the mathematical guarantee that “an oscillator will always start up and settle into steady oscillation”

5. The Describing Function Method

In practice, the steady-state amplitude of an oscillator is usually analysed with the describing function , which linearizes the nonlinear element into anamplitude-dependent gain $ N(A) $.

Steady-state oscillation condition

$$ N(A_0) \cdot H(j\omega_0) = 1 $$

Stability criterion for the limit cycle

$$ \left.\frac{\partial |NH|}{\partial A}\right|_{A=A_0} < 0 \quad \Rightarrow \text{stable limit cycle} $$

Physical meaning:the loop gain must fall as the amplitude grows, so that a negative-feedback mechanism pulls the amplitude back to $ A_0 $.

7. Connections to Radar / RF Systems

  1. Phase-noise analysis: Leeson's model is essentially a perturbation analysis of a limit cycle disturbed by noise — that is,linearization about the limit cycle(linearize around the limit cycle, not around the origin).
  2. Injection locking: an external signal injected into the oscillator locks it. This can only be analysed with nonlinear methods (Adler's equation); LTI fails completely.
  3. PLL locking dynamics: once locked, the small-signal behaviour can be analysed with LTI loop dynamics, butlarge-signal acquisition and cycle slipping must go back to nonlinear analysis.
  4. Saturation behaviour of mixers / PAs: another application of the describing function.

8. Summary

Core idea:
The design goal of an oscillator is to make the “origin” LTI-unstable while making the “limit cycle” nonlinearly stable.

The two notions of stability describe differentinvariant sets:
  • Bode / PM / GM → look at theorigin
  • Van der Pol / Lyapunov / describing function → look at the limit cycle

Design-flow quick reference

  1. Small-signal stage(start-up): design for $ |AH| \gg 1 $ with the correct phase → “unstable” in the LTI sense, so that noise is amplified exponentially
  2. Large-signal stage(steady state): nonlinearity makes $ g_m $ drop automatically, $ |NH| = 1 $ → convergence to a stable limit cycle
  3. Perturbation analysis(phase noise): linearize about the limit cycle and apply the Leeson model
Oscillator stability summary · linear feedback ↔ nonlinear limit cycle · applies to LC VCO / crystal oscillator / ring oscillator / radar LO design

RF oscillator circuits typically use a BJT/FET in common-emitter/source, common-base/gate, or common-collector/drain configurations, paired with a feedback network. The four classical topologies that result are the Hartley, Colpitts, Clapp, and Pierce oscillators.

5.1.3 General Transistor Oscillator Analysis

For a unilateral (i.e. $S_{12}=0$, no reverse transmission, so the output does not feed back into the input) transistor model with four voltage nodes $V_1$ (input), $V_2$ (common), $V_3$ (feedback), $V_4$ (output) — connect node $V_3$ to $V_4$ to provide feedback. Apply KCL at all four nodes to obtain the admittance matrix equation:

$$\begin{pmatrix} (Y_1+Y_3+G_i) & -(Y_1+G_i) & -Y_3 & 0 \\ -(Y_1+G_i+g_m) & (Y_1+Y_2+G_i+G_o+g_m) & -Y_2 & -G_o \\ -Y_3 & -Y_2 & (Y_2+Y_3) & 0 \\ -g_m & -(G_o+g_m) & 0 & G_o \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}V_1\\V_2\\V_3\\V_4\end{pmatrix}=0$$

For the common emitter/source configuration: ground node $V_2$ ($V_2=0$), connect feedback ($V_4=V_3=V$), and assume negligible output conductance ($G_o\approx 0$). The matrix reduces to a 2×2 system:

$$\begin{pmatrix} (Y_1+Y_3+G_i) & -Y_3 \\ -(g_m+Y_3) & (Y_2+Y_3) \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}V_1\\V\end{pmatrix}=0$$

For non-trivial solutions, the determinant must vanish. Substituting $Y_k = jB_k$ (purely reactive feedback elements):

Why “a non-trivial solution ⟺ determinant = 0” (click to collapse)

The nodal equations arehomogeneous(the right-hand side is 0, with no applied source): $[Y]\,\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{0}$.

  • A homogeneous system always has the trivial solution $\mathbf{V}=\mathbf{0}$ (all node voltages zero = the circuit is not oscillating).
  • Oscillation is a non-trivial solution $\mathbf{V}\neq\mathbf{0}$:— finite node voltages with zero input, i.e. self-sustaining.
  • Linear algebra: $\det[Y]\neq0 \Rightarrow [Y]$ is invertible $\Rightarrow$ the unique solution is $\mathbf{V}=[Y]^{-1}\mathbf{0}=\mathbf{0}$ (trivial only); $\det[Y]=0 \Rightarrow$ the matrix issingularwith a non-trivial null space $\Rightarrow$ infinitely many non-zero solutions exist.

So “there must be a non-zero solution” $\Leftrightarrow$ the matrix is singular $\Leftrightarrow \boxed{\det[Y]=0}$. That is theOscillation conditionitself, equivalent to $1-AH=0$ in feedback form (a vanishing denominator → a finite output with zero input). Splitting the complex determinant into $\mathrm{Re}\{\det\}=0$ and $\mathrm{Im}\{\det\}=0$ solves simultaneously forthe oscillation frequency $\omega_0$(the phase condition) andthe transconductance $g_m$ required(the gain condition) — exactly the basis for deriving Hartley/Colpitts from the 2×2 determinant below.

It oscillates with no applied source — does that depend on the initial conditions? (click to expand)

You need to keep three things distinct —start-up, amplitude and phase— because each relates differently to the initial conditions (IC).

① Start-up: a disturbance is needed, but not an “applied source”. $\det=0$ is the ideal steady-state condition (poles on the $j\omega$ axis); a real oscillator is designed so that $|AH|>1$ at start-up (poles in the RHP). Thermal noise, shot noise and the power-on transient are themselves the IC, and the RHP poles amplifyAnyany tiny disturbance exponentially as $e^{\sigma t}$ → there is always noise, so it always starts. The IC is needed to “light the fire”, but its content does not matter.

② Amplitude: locked by the nonlinearity, independent of the IC. In the ideal linear case (poles exactly on the $j\omega$ axis, marginal) it behaves like an ideal LC and the amplitude really would be “set by the IC” and never change. In a real circuit $g_m$ compresses with amplitude → convergence to a stable limit cycle: whatever the IC, it converges tothe same steady-state amplitude(the Van der Pol statement above that “every non-zero starting point converges to $\Gamma$”).

③ Phase: this is the quantity genuinely determined by the IC / noise. An oscillator is anautonomous systemwith no external time reference → it has no preferred phase. The absolute phase is set by the noise that lit the fire and is arbitrary; the limit cycle isneutrally stablein the phase direction, and noise keeps nudging it → the phase performs a random walk = phase noise(Leeson).

The mathematical connection to $\det=0$
$\det[Y]=0$ ⇒ the null space is one-dimensional ⇒ the solution is determined only up to a complex scalar multiple $\mathbf{V}=c\,\mathbf{v}_0$, where $c=$ amplitude $\times\,e^{j\phi}$. Linear theory gives onlythe relative amplitude/phase between nodes (the mode shape $\mathbf{v}_0$)and cannot fix $c$:the magnitude $|c|$ is fixed by the nonlinearity, while the phase $\angle c$ is left permanently free to the IC/noiseof the image-reject mixer at low IF.
In one sentence:start-up is lit by noise (any non-zero IC will do), the amplitude is locked by the nonlinearity (independent of the IC), and only the phase is genuinely set by the IC/noise — and it drifts freelyof the image-reject mixer at low IF.
$$\det = (G_i+j(B_1+B_3))\cdot j(B_2+B_3) - (-jB_3)(-(g_m+jB_3)) = 0$$

Separating real and imaginary parts and letting $X_k = 1/B_k$, the oscillation conditions become:

Two oscillation conditions $$X_1 + X_2 + X_3 = 0 \quad\text{(phase / frequency)}$$ $$g_m = G_i\,\frac{X_2+X_3}{X_2}\quad\xrightarrow{\;X_2+X_3=-X_1\;}\quad \frac{X_1}{X_2}= -\frac{g_m}{G_i}\quad\text{(gain)}$$
How the two conditions follow from $\det=0$ (including the ratio form) — click to expand

$\det=0$ isa single complex equation → setting the real and imaginary parts each to 0 gives two conditions:

$$\text{Re}:\;-(B_1{+}B_3)(B_2{+}B_3)+B_3^2=0\;\xrightarrow{\;\div\,B_1B_2B_3,\ X_k=1/B_k\;}\;X_1+X_2+X_3=0\ \text{(phase)}$$ $$\text{Im}:\;G_i(B_2{+}B_3)-g_mB_3=0\ \text{(gain, original form)}$$

Recasting the gain condition in terms of $X$: Substituting $B_k=1/X_k$ into the imaginary part:

$$G_i\Big(\frac1{X_2}+\frac1{X_3}\Big)=\frac{g_m}{X_3}\;\xrightarrow{\;\times X_3\;}\;G_i\frac{X_2+X_3}{X_2}=g_m$$

Ratio form: Then substituting the phase condition $X_1+X_2+X_3=0\Rightarrow X_2+X_3=-X_1$:

$$G_i\frac{-X_1}{X_2}=g_m\;\Longrightarrow\;\boxed{\frac{X_1}{X_2}=-\frac{g_m}{G_i}}$$

$g_m,G_i>0$ ⇒ the ratio is negative, which fixes the relative signs of $X_1$ and $X_2$ — and that is what distinguishes Colpitts from Hartley.

Note
that the gain conditioncannotbe written as $X_3=-\frac{g_m}{G_i}(X_1+X_2)$: substituting the phase condition $X_1+X_2=-X_3$ collapses it into the contradiction $g_m=G_i$. The correct form is the $g_m=G_i\frac{X_2+X_3}{X_2}$ above (or the ratio form).
Reading the condition
The first condition $X_1+X_2+X_3=0$ means the three reactances cannot all be the same sign — two must be one type (e.g., both capacitors) and the third must be opposite (an inductor). The sign assignment determines whether the circuit is Colpitts or Hartley.

5.1.4 Classical Oscillator Topologies

❓ Prerequisite: LC resonance — click to expand

The frequency of every LC oscillator is set by LC resonance . Resonance = energy sloshing back and forth between the inductance $L$ and the capacitance $C$.

The physical picture:

  • The capacitor $C$ stores energy in theelectric field: $E = \tfrac12 CV^2$
  • The inductor $L$ stores energy in themagnetic field: $E = \tfrac12 LI^2$
  • At resonance the energy is exchanged back and forth between the two (like potential ↔ kinetic energy in a pendulum): C discharges → current flows through L building up a magnetic field → L then charges C the other way → and round it goes.

Resonant frequency(when $\omega L = 1/\omega C$, the two reactances being equal in magnitude):

$$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$

Two kinds of LC resonant circuit:

By contrast:Series resonance is “Currentheaven” (low impedance, large current); parallel resonance is “Voltageheaven” (high impedance, large voltage swing, circulating current inside the tank). The animation speed can be adjusted for all four circuits together with the slider above ($\,$--ac-dur$\,$).

The Q factor (quality factor) — the sharpness of the resonance:

$$Q = \frac{\omega_0 L}{R} = \frac{1}{\omega_0 R C} = \frac{f_0}{\mathrm{BW_{3dB}}}$$

High $Q$ → a narrow, sharp resonant peak, low loss, stable frequency and low phase noise. This is exactly where the $\propto 1/Q^2$ dependence of phase noise in Leeson's formula comes from (see 5.1.9).

Relevance to this section:Colpitts resonates $C_\text{eq}=C_1C_2/(C_1+C_2)$ with $L_3$; Hartley resonates $(L_1+L_2)$ with $C_3$; Pierce treats the crystal as an equivalent inductance resonating with $C_1,C_2$. The frequency formula of every topology below is essentially a variation on $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{L_\text{eq}C_\text{eq}}$.

Colpitts oscillator — two capacitors + one inductor

Let $X_1 = -1/\omega_0 C_1$, $X_2 = -1/\omega_0 C_2$, $X_3 = \omega_0 L_3$. The phase condition gives the oscillation frequency:

Colpitts frequency $$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L_3\,C_{\text{eq}}}}, \quad C_{\text{eq}} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1+C_2}$$

And the magnitude condition gives the required device transconductance:

$$\frac{C_2}{C_1} = \frac{g_m}{G_i}$$

Hartley oscillator — two inductors + one capacitor

Let $X_1 = \omega_0 L_1$, $X_2 = \omega_0 L_2$, $X_3 = -1/\omega_0 C_3$:

Hartley frequency $$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(L_1+L_2)\,C_3}}$$ $$\frac{L_1}{L_2} = \frac{g_m}{G_i}$$

Pierce (crystal) oscillator

Based on the common-emitter Colpitts. The inductor $L_3$ is replaced by a crystal operating between its series and parallel resonant frequencies (so it looks inductive). The two capacitors $C_1, C_2$ are connected from the base and collector to ground; the crystal connects between base and collector. The crystal's high $Q$ (10⁴–10⁶) gives outstanding frequency stability (a few ppm).

Clapp oscillator

Based on the common-gate Colpitts. Adds a series capacitor $C_3$ in series with the inductor $L_3$ to reduce the effective inductance value and thus the device's parasitic-capacitance loading on the tuning. Better frequency stability than basic Colpitts when high $Q$ is needed.

Detailed schematics + a plain-language explanation (as if explaining to a high-school student)

First, what an oscillator is actually doing (think of a playground swing)
An LC circuit is a swing. Energy sloshes back and forth between the inductance $L$ and the capacitance $C$ (magnetic field ↔ electric field), swinging at its own fixed rhythm: $f_0=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$ — and that sets thefrequencyof the image-reject mixer at low IF.
But a swing gradually stops because of friction (the circuit hasloss).The transistoris the person who “gives it a little push at the right moment”: on every swing it tops up a little energy from the supply $V_{CC}$, exactly making up the loss, so the swing keeps going atconstant amplitude.
Only two things are needed for this to work: (1) push hard enough(gain ≥ loss, = the start-up condition); and (2) push at the right moment(phase alignment = positive feedback).
The four oscillators below differonlyin “what components make the swing, and how a small part of the signal is fed back to the transistor to tell it *when* to push”. $V_{CC}$, $R_1/R_2$ (bias) and $R_E$ are the same in all four figures — the DC settings that keep the transistor “alive”; what really distinguishes them isthe red/green feedback networkof the image-reject mixer at low IF.
In the four figures below, theorange dots ● = are the AC current, whichflows back and fortharound the LC loop(forwards a little, backwards a little) — the very picture of a swing going to and fro.
Before looking at the circuits, meet the BJT (think of a tap) — click to expand
A BJT = a three-legged amplifier
A BJT (bipolar junction transistor) has three legs:base (B), collector (C) and emitter (E). The commonest type is NPN.
The idea in one sentence:a very small base current $i_B$ controls a very large collector current $i_C$($i_C=\beta\,i_B$, with $\beta$ often ~100). Just like atap— a light turn of the handle (the small signal at B) controls a large flow of water (the large current from C to E). This “small controls large” isamplificationof the image-reject mixer at low IF.
In an oscillator, the BJT is the one who“tops up the energy and pushes the swing”: it converts DC energy from the supply $V_{CC}$, under the direction of the feedback signal, into exactly the right AC push to sustain the LC swing.

Biasing and configurations: $R_1/R_2$ first set B at a “half-open” operating point so that $i_C$ can swing up and down with the signal, while $R_E$ stabilizes that operating point. Which leg serves as the common reference gives three configurations:common-emitter CE(in at B, out at C, the highest gain and by far the most common), common-base (CB) and common-collector (CC). Colpitts, Hartley and Pierce below all use CE; Clapp uses the FET equivalent, the common-gateof the image-reject mixer at low IF.

In plain terms: $L_3$ and the two capacitors form the swing. The transistor watches the base (the voltage on $C_1$) to decide when to push; the strength of the push is set by the ratio of $C_1$ to $C_2$(like where along the rope you grab the swing). It is the commonest and easiest LC oscillator to build.

How the full circuit reduces to the AC equivalent (the topological skeleton) — click to expand

Reduce the full biased circuit to just the skeleton that mattersto AC. The rules (all with respect to the AC signal):

  1. The DC supply $V_{CC}$ → grounded. $V_{CC}$ is a fixed DC level, and to a varying AC signal it is just as immovable as ground → treat it as an AC short to ground. The whole $V_{CC}$ rail becomes “ground”.
  2. Bypass/coupling capacitors → short circuits(their impedance at the operating frequency is ≈ 0, so treat them as wires).
  3. The bias resistors $R_1,R_2$ and the RF choke → removed(high impedance, they only set the DC operating point and barely affect the LC loop).
  4. $R_E$ (bypassed) → the emitter goes to AC ground.

What remains: the transistor plus $L_3,C_1,C_2$ = the skeleton. The key point is thatstep ① turns the $V_{CC}$ rail into AC ground, and itmerges with the bottom ground into a single node → so $C_2$ and $L_3$, originally connected to the $V_{CC}$ rail, become grounded just like $C_1$.

This also explains the animation:the orange dots (the AC current) only circulate in the $L_3$–$C_1$–$C_2$ loop, because everything else ($V_{CC}$, the bias) is either “immovable / high impedance / a short circuit” to AC — the only thing genuinely swinging back and forth is this tank.

How do $L_3$ and $C_1,C_2$ become a series/parallel tank? (click here if it is not obvious)

The key point:$C_1$ and $C_2$ are in series with each other, and that series capacitance is then in parallel with $L_3$. The AC equivalent makes it clear:

Why $C_1$ and $C_2$ are in series rather than in parallel:they shareonly one point at the bottom (ground), while their tops go to different nodes ($C_2$→collector, $C_1$→base); the current goes in through one and out through the other → in series. Ground is thetap (midpoint)between them. (For a parallel connectionboth endswould have to meet at the same two points.)

Two equivalent ways of seeing it:

  • By loop (series):the current follows a single loop, collector $\xrightarrow{L_3}$ base $\xrightarrow{C_1}$ ground $\xrightarrow{C_2}$ collector, passing in turn through $L_3\to C_1\to C_2$ = in series (this is the loop the orange dots trace in the animation).
  • By terminals (parallel):between collector and base there are two paths: (a) $L_3$, and (b) $C_2$ in series with $C_1$ → hence $L_3 \parallel (C_1\text{ in series with }C_2)$ = a parallel LC tank.

At resonance the capacitance seen by $L_3$ is the series value:

$$C_s=\frac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2},\qquad f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_3\,C_s}}$$

In plain terms: take Colpitts' “two capacitors, one inductor” and swap the roles entirely to give “two inductors, one capacitor”. The amount of feedback is set by the ratio of $L_1$ to $L_2$ . A single coil with a centre tap is commonly used; turning the core tunes the frequency, which makes wide tuning ranges very convenient.

How is the Hartley tank put together? (series/parallel)

Hartley is thedualof Colpitts: swap “two capacitors, one inductor” for “two inductors, one capacitor”. The reduction rules ($V_{CC}$→ground, etc.) are the same as for Colpitts.

In plain terms:$L_1$ and $L_2$ share ground at the bottom (the midpoint) → they are in series(that is, a centre-tapped coil); that series inductance $(L_1{+}L_2)$ is then in parallel withwith $C_3$ to form the tank. The feedback ratio is set by $L_1:L_2$.

$$f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(L_1+L_2)\,C_3}}$$

In plain terms: a quartz crystal is equivalent to an LC with an extraordinarily high $Q$ (tens of thousands to millions)— like a swing that hardly ever stops and keeps superb time. So the frequency is extremely stable (ppm-level, barely drifting with temperature). Your watch and the clocks in your computer/MCU almost all use one.

How is the Pierce tank put together? (series/parallel)

Exactly the same as Colpitts, except that $L_3$ is replaced by aquartz crystal. Between its series and parallel resonances the crystal isinductive, equivalent to an inductor of extremely high $Q$. The reduction rules are as for Colpitts.

In plain terms:$C_1$ and $C_2$ share ground (the midpoint) → they are in series, and with the crystal (inductive) they arein parallel withto form the tank, the same shape as Colpitts. The difference is the crystal's enormous $Q$ (tens of thousands to millions) → itlocks the frequency extremely tightly(to ppm), with almost no drift over temperature.

In plain terms: the transistor's parasitic capacitances wander with temperature and operating point, making a Colpitts oscillator's frequency drift. Clapp puts avery small, very stable $C_3$ in series with the inductor to “dominate” the frequency, therebydiluting away the influence of those unstable transistor capacitances → a more stable frequency.

How is the Clapp tank put together? (series/parallel)

It is Colpitts, with the top-edge element changed from a plain $L_3$ to $L_3$ in series with a small $C_3$. The reduction rules are as for Colpitts.

In plain terms:the tank = $(L_3\text{ in series with }C_3)$ and $(C_1\text{ in series with }C_2)$ in parallel with. Since $C_3 \ll C_1,C_2$, the total series capacitance isdominated by the very small, very stable $C_3$ → this dilutes the influence of the transistor's parasitic capacitances and the frequency is more stable.

$$f_0\approx\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_3\,C_\text{tot}}}\quad(C_\text{tot}\text{ dominated by }C_3)$$

The only differences are “which positions hold capacitors and which holds the inductor (or crystal)”, and which transistor configuration is used (common-emitter CE / common-gate CG).

From the Barkhausen oscillation condition (loop gain $=1\angle 0°$), a common-emitter amplifier (which itself supplies $180°$ of phase shift) yields two conditions:

The phase (frequency) condition and the amplitude condition $$X_1 + X_2 + X_3 = 0, \qquad \frac{X_1}{X_2} = A_v \;(>0)$$
The sign rule → deciding whether it is Colpitts or Hartley
$X_1+X_2+X_3=0$ means the three reactances cannot all have the same sign:$X_1$ and $X_2$ must be of the same kind (same sign) and $X_3$ the oppositeof the image-reject mixer at low IF.
  • $X_1,X_2$ arecapacitor(negative reactance) and $X_3$ is aninductors(positive reactance) → Colpitts(a capacitive divider). Pierce and Clapp both belong to this family.
  • $X_1,X_2$ areinductors(positive reactance) and $X_3$ is acapacitor(negative reactance) → Hartley(an inductive divider).
The feedback ratio is set by $X_1/X_2$ (i.e. $C_1/C_2$ or $L_1/L_2$), and it must be large enough to satisfy the amplitude condition and start up.

A. Common-emitter Colpitts (capacitive divider)

Configuration:common-emitter (CE). The tank is $L_3$ in parallel with “$C_1$ in series with $C_2$”; the junction between $C_1$ and $C_2$ is thecapacitive-dividertap, returning part of the output voltage to the input (the base).

Oscillation frequency $$\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_3\,C_\text{eq}}},\quad C_\text{eq}=\frac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}$$
Start-up (amplitude) condition $$\frac{C_2}{C_1}=\frac{g_m}{G_i}$$

Characteristics:a capacitive divider is easy to build and insensitive to stray inductance, making thisthe most widely used LC oscillator. It works from VHF/UHF up to GHz and is the basis of the VCO (simply replace $C$ with a varactor to make it tunable).

B. Common-emitter Hartley (inductive divider)

Configuration:common-emitter (CE). The tank is $C_3$ in parallel with “$L_1$ in series with $L_2$”; atapped inductorprovides inductive-divider feedback — which is exactly where it is the dual of Colpitts: capacitors and inductors are interchanged.

Oscillation frequency $$\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(L_1+L_2)\,C_3}}$$
Start-up (amplitude) condition $$\frac{L_1}{L_2}=\frac{g_m}{G_i}$$

Characteristics:a single variable capacitor $C_3$ giveswide-range tuning, which is why it was common in early radios and signal generators. Its drawbacks: a tapped inductor is bulky and hard to integrate on an IC, it is rather sensitive to stray capacitance, and its frequency is usually lower than Colpitts.

C. Pierce (crystal oscillator)

Configuration:based on thecommon-emitter Colpitts , with the resonant inductor $L_3$ replaced by aquartz crystal. The crystal is inductivebetween its series resonance $f_s$ and its parallel resonance $f_p$, which is exactly the role $L_3$ played; $C_1,C_2$ still provide the capacitive-divider feedback.

Crystal equivalent (a high-Q LC) $$Q_\text{xtal}\sim 10^4\text{–}10^6 \;\gg\; Q_\text{LC}\;(\sim10^2)$$

Characteristics:the crystal can bemodelled as an LC resonator of extremely high $Q$, so the frequency stability reaches few ppm, far better than a pure LC type. The price is that the frequency is essentiallyfixed(only slight pulling is possible) and is determined by how the crystal is cut.

Uses: microprocessor/MCU clocks, frequency references and communication reference sources. Since Leeson's formula gives phase noise $\propto 1/Q^2$, an extremely high $Q$ also brings extremely low phase noise.

D. Clapp (the series-capacitor improvement)

Configuration:based on thecommon-gate (CG) Colpitts , with asmall capacitor $C_3$ added in serieswith the inductor $L_3$. The resonance is set by $L_3$ together with the series value of the three capacitors:

Oscillation frequency $$\omega_0=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L_3\,C_\text{tot}}},\quad \frac{1}{C_\text{tot}}=\frac{1}{C_1}+\frac{1}{C_2}+\frac{1}{C_3}$$

Because $C_3\ll C_1,C_2$, $C_\text{tot}\approx C_3$. The transistor'sparasitic capacitances appear in parallel with $C_1,C_2$, whereas the frequency is set mainly by $L_3$ and $C_3$ → the effect of parasitic variation on the frequency is heavilydilutedof the image-reject mixer at low IF.

Why is Clapp more stable than Colpitts?
A basic Colpitts' frequency involves $C_\text{eq}=C_1C_2/(C_1+C_2)$, and the transistor's parasitic capacitances sit directly across $C_1,C_2$, drifting with temperature and bias. Clapp lets $C_3$ (small, stable and external) dominate the frequency, so $\omega_0$ depends almost entirely on $L_3C_3$ → better stability. The price: a narrower tuning range (change $C_3$ and everything changes).

5.1.5 RF Oscillator Model — One-Port Negative-Resistance Device

An alternative view: the active device acts as a one-port with input impedance

$$Z_\text{in}(I,s) = R_\text{in}(I,s) + jX_\text{in}(I,s), \quad R_\text{in} < 0$$
Why is $R_\text{in}$ written as a function of two variables $(I,\,j\omega)$? (click to expand)

$R_\text{in}$ is thereal partof the device's input impedance, and it is written as a function of boththe current amplitude $I$ andthe frequency $\omega$ , each governing one thing:

① Dependence on the amplitude $I$ → because the device is nonlinear (large-signal).This is the key to amplitude stability:

  • Start-up: $|R_\text{in}|>R_L$ ($R_\text{in}$ strongly negative) → net negative resistance → the amplitude grows exponentially.
  • As the amplitude $I$ grows → $R_\text{in}$ becomesless negativeof the image-reject mixer at low IF.
  • to $I_0$: $R_\text{in}(I_0,\omega_0)+R_L=0$ → the net resistance vanishes → it settles at thesteady-state amplitude(a limit cycle). The corresponding stability condition is $\partial R_\text{in}/\partial I>0$.

This is the negative-resistance version of “$g_m$ compressing with amplitude”.

② Dependence on the frequency $\omega$ → together with $X_\text{in}(I,j\omega)$, it sets the oscillation frequency $\omega_0$ at the frequency where $X_\text{in}+X_L=0$.

The point:a linear small-signal model looks only at a single operating point andcannot see the $I$ dependence → so it cannot explain why the amplitude settles by itself; only by writing $R_\text{in}(I,j\omega)$ do you capture both “amplitude locking” and “frequency setting”.

connected to a passive load $Z_L = R_L + jX_L$. Applying KVL:

Oscillation condition (sum of impedances = 0) $$Z_\text{in} + Z_L = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \begin{cases}R_L + R_\text{in} = 0 \\ X_L + X_\text{in} = 0\end{cases}$$

Equivalent form using reflection coefficients:

$$\bbox[#f8c9c0,5px]{\Gamma_L = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} = -\frac{Z_\text{in}-Z_0}{Z_\text{in}+Z_0}\cdot\frac{Z_0}{Z_0}=\frac{1}{\Gamma_\text{in}}}$$ $$\bbox[#f8c9c0,5px]{\boxed{\;\Gamma_L\,\Gamma_\text{in} = 1\;}}$$

How oscillation builds up

  1. At small signal $I\approx 0$: $R_\text{in}(0,j\omega) + R_L < 0$ → system is unstable, any noise/transient grows.
  2. As current $I$ increases, $R_\text{in}(I,j\omega)$ becomes less negative (nonlinear compression).
  3. At steady current $I_0$: $R_\text{in}(I_0,j\omega_0) + R_L = 0$ and $X_\text{in}(I_0,j\omega_0) + X_L(j\omega_0) = 0$.
  4. Oscillator runs in a stable state at frequency $\omega_0$.
Physical intuition
The active device pumps in energy at the rate $-R_\text{in}|I|^2$, exactly cancelling the energy dissipated in $R_L|I|^2$. Net loss per cycle = 0 → sustained oscillation.

5.1.6 Stable Oscillation Conditions (Kurokawa)

Stable oscillation requires that any perturbation in current or frequency decays back to the original state. Starting from $Z_T(I,s)=Z_\text{in}(I,s)+Z_L(s)=0$, expand in Taylor series about $(I_0,s_0)$:

$$Z_T(I,s) = Z_T(I_0,s_0) + \left.\frac{\partial Z_T}{\partial s}\right|_{(I_0,s_0)}\!\delta s \;+\; \left.\frac{\partial Z_T}{\partial I}\right|_{(I_0,s_0)}\!\delta I \;=\; 0$$

Note that $Z_T(I_0,s_0)=0$, representing the original state of oscillation. Solving for $\delta s$:

$$\therefore\;\delta s = -\left.\frac{\partial Z_T/\partial I}{\partial Z_T/\partial s}\right|_{(I_0,s_0)}\!\delta I \;\overset{s=j\omega}{=}\; -\left.\frac{(\partial Z_T/\partial I)\cdot \bbox[#f8c9c0,3px]{j\,(\partial Z_T^*/\partial\omega)}}{|\partial Z_T/\partial\omega|^2}\right|_{(I_0,\omega_0)}\!\delta I$$
The second equality: where do the $j$ and the conjugate $Z_T^*$ come from? (click to expand)

① The chain rule (changing variable to $s=j\omega$) → produces the $j$. Along the $j\omega$ axis, one step in $s$ = one step in $j\,\omega$:

$$\frac{\partial Z_T}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial Z_T}{\partial (j\omega)}=\frac{1}{j}\frac{\partial Z_T}{\partial \omega}=-j\,\frac{\partial Z_T}{\partial \omega}\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{1}{\partial Z_T/\partial s}=\frac{j}{\partial Z_T/\partial \omega}$$

② Rationalizing (multiplying by the conjugate of the denominator) → produces the $Z_T^*$. The denominator $\partial Z_T/\partial\omega$ iscomplex, so you cannot read off the real and imaginary parts of $\delta s$ directly. The standard manoeuvre: multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, turning the denominator into arealsquared modulus:

$$\frac{j}{\partial Z_T/\partial \omega}\cdot\frac{(\partial Z_T/\partial \omega)^*}{(\partial Z_T/\partial \omega)^*}=\frac{j\,(\partial Z_T^*/\partial \omega)}{|\partial Z_T/\partial \omega|^2}$$

Why this is unavoidable:next we set $\delta s=\alpha+j\,\delta\omega$ and ask whether the transient $e^{\delta s\,t}$ decays (by the sign of $\alpha$). Only once the denominator is real ($|\cdot|^2$), so that all the complexity is concentrated in the numerator, can the real part $\alpha$ of $\delta s$ be extracted cleanly — take $\mathrm{Re}\{\}$ and the Kurokawa condition follows directly. The conjugate here ispurely an algebraic device(rationalization),a different origin from the “take the in-phase component physically” of the Ch4 power formula $\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}\{VI^*\}$, though both are ways of “turning a complex number into a real one”of the image-reject mixer at low IF.

Let $\delta s = \alpha + j\,\delta\omega$. For the transient $e^{\delta s\,t} = e^{j\,\delta\omega\, t}\,e^{\alpha t}$ to decay, we need $\alpha < 0$. Taking the real part:

Stable oscillation condition (Kurokawa) $$\frac{\partial R_T}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{\partial X_T}{\partial \omega} \;-\; \frac{\partial X_T}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{\partial R_T}{\partial \omega} \;>\; 0$$
No need to memorize it — it is simply the real part of the expression above (two lines of algebra, click to expand)

Let $Z_T=R_T+jX_T$ and abbreviate $A=\dfrac{\partial R_T}{\partial I},\;B=\dfrac{\partial X_T}{\partial I},\;C=\dfrac{\partial R_T}{\partial \omega},\;D=\dfrac{\partial X_T}{\partial \omega}$.

① Expand the complex product in the numerator(noting that $\partial Z_T^*/\partial\omega = C - jD$):

$$\frac{\partial Z_T}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{\partial Z_T^*}{\partial \omega}=(A+jB)(C-jD)=(AC+BD)+j(BC-AD)$$

② Multiply by $j$, substitute back into $\delta s$ and take the real part(multiplying by $j$ swaps real and imaginary: $(AD-BC)+j(AC+BD)$):

$$\delta s=-\frac{(AD-BC)+j(AC+BD)}{|\partial Z_T/\partial \omega|^2}\,\delta I\quad\Rightarrow\quad \alpha=\mathrm{Re}\{\delta s\}=-\frac{AD-BC}{|\partial Z_T/\partial \omega|^2}\,\delta I$$

③ Demand recovery:when the amplitude overshoots, $\delta I>0$, the transient must decay ($\alpha<0$); the denominator is a positive real number, so

$$\alpha<0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; AD-BC>0 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \frac{\partial R_T}{\partial I}\frac{\partial X_T}{\partial \omega}-\frac{\partial X_T}{\partial I}\frac{\partial R_T}{\partial \omega}>0$$

(the converse also holds: for $\delta I<0$, $\alpha>0$ pushes the amplitude back up — the same condition, recovering in both directions.)

A mnemonic
If you must memorize it, remember it as the Jacobian determinant being positive: $\;\det\dfrac{\partial(R_T,X_T)}{\partial(I,\omega)}=\begin{vmatrix}\partial R_T/\partial I & \partial R_T/\partial \omega\\[2pt] \partial X_T/\partial I & \partial X_T/\partial \omega\end{vmatrix}>0$ — the 2×2 cross-multiplication is exactly the expression above.

For a passive load: $\partial R_L/\partial I = 0$, $\partial X_L/\partial I = 0$, $\partial R_L/\partial\omega = 0$, so the condition reduces to:

$$\frac{\partial R_\text{in}}{\partial I}\bigg|_{I_0}\!\!\cdot\frac{\partial(X_L+X_\text{in})}{\partial\omega}\bigg|_{\omega_0} \;>\; 0$$
How this is simplified, and what each factor governs (click to expand)

The simplification. $R_T=R_\text{in}+R_L$ and $X_T=X_\text{in}+X_L$. The load is passive → it does not vary with thedevice current($\partial R_L/\partial I=\partial X_L/\partial I=0$; only a nonlinear active element would), and we assume $\partial R_L/\partial\omega=0$ (in a resonator it is mainly the reactance that moves). Hence $\partial R_T/\partial I=\partial R_\text{in}/\partial I$ and $\partial X_T/\partial\omega=\partial(X_L+X_\text{in})/\partial\omega$, and Kurokawa becomes:

$$\underbrace{\frac{\partial R_\text{in}}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{\partial(X_L+X_\text{in})}{\partial\omega}}_{\text{first term}}\;-\;\underbrace{\frac{\partial X_\text{in}}{\partial I}\cdot\frac{\partial R_\text{in}}{\partial\omega}}_{\text{second term}}\;>\;0$$

Why the second term can be dropped (the high-Q approximation). The second term ispurely a device term: both factors involve only the device, and the device's $R_\text{in},X_\text{in}$ are broadband, smooth functions (with no resonance of their own) → small slopes, bounded magnitudes, andindependent of $Q$. The first term, however, contains $\partial X_L/\partial\omega$, and the resonator reactance crosses zero sharply at $\omega_0$ with a slope $\propto Q$ (for a series RLC: $\partial X/\partial\omega|_{\omega_0}=2L=2QR/\omega_0$). The ratio of the two terms is $\sim\partial X_L/\partial\omega\,/\,$the device slope $\propto Q$ → at high Q the first term ≫ the second, so dropping the second term is a high-Q approximation and not an identity (a low-Q load requires the full Kurokawa condition).

① $\partial R_\text{in}/\partial I\big|_{I_0}>0$ — the amplitude is self-stabilizing. $R_\text{in}$ becomesless negativeas the current grows (gain compression). If the amplitude overshoots → the net resistance $R_\text{in}+R_L$ turns from 0 to positive → damping pulls it back to $I_0$; if the amplitude falls → the net resistance turns negative → it is topped back up (a limit cycle).

② $\partial(X_L+X_\text{in})/\partial\omega\big|_{\omega_0}>0$ — the frequency is self-stabilizing. The total reactance crosses zero at $\omega_0$ with apositive slope: if the frequency drifts → the total reactance ≠ 0 → the phase condition is violated → the frequency is pushed back to $\omega_0$. The steeper the slope, the more firmly the frequency is pinned.

Why this leads to “use a high-Q resonator”
The reactance slope $\partial X/\partial\omega\propto Q$. The steeper the slope of $X_L$ → the more easily the condition is met and the stronger the frequency restoring force → the lower the phase noise (which reconnects with Leeson). This is the mathematical basis for “using a high-$Q$ resonator such as a cavity or dielectric resonator for stable oscillation”.
In one sentence: the first term governs “the amplitude coming back by itself” and the second “the frequency coming back by itself”; with both positive, steady oscillation is a stable equilibrium point.
Two practical consequences
  • Gain compression: $R_\text{in}$ must become less negative as $I$ grows, i.e. $\partial R_\text{in}/\partial I > 0$.
  • Steep reactance slope (high $Q$): The larger $\partial X_L/\partial\omega$, the more easily the condition is satisfied → use high-$Q$ resonators (cavity, dielectric, crystal) for the most stable oscillation.

Effective oscillator design also requires considerations of: operating point for max power and stability, frequency pulling (load impedance sensitivity), large-signal device effects, and noise.

5.1.7 Two-Port Transistor Oscillator Circuit

A practical transistor oscillator is a potentially-unstable two-port terminated so that the input port presents a negative resistance. The input is connected to a load (tank) network L for tuning, and the output to a terminating network T for power delivery.

Reflection coefficients $$\Gamma_\text{in} = S_{11} + \frac{S_{12}S_{21}\Gamma_T}{1-S_{22}\Gamma_T}, \qquad \Gamma_\text{out} = S_{22} + \frac{S_{12}S_{21}\Gamma_L}{1-S_{11}\Gamma_L}$$ $$\boxed{\;\Gamma_\text{in}\,\Gamma_L = 1 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \Gamma_\text{out}\,\Gamma_T = 1\;}$$
“$=1$” is a complex equation = two conditions: $|\Gamma_\text{in}||\Gamma_L|=1$ → one trip round the loop neither grows nor shrinks the amplitude(what the tank absorbs is exactly replaced by the negative resistance; step by step it is still $|\Gamma_L|<1$ shrinking and $|\Gamma_\text{in}|>1$ growing); and $\angle\Gamma_\text{in}+\angle\Gamma_L=0°$ → the phases line up exactly (picking out $\omega_0$). At start-up the product is $>1$ and grows each lap, and saturation compresses $|\Gamma_\text{in}(A)|$ back to the steady state where the product is exactly $=1$

Where does $\Gamma_{in}\Gamma_L=1$ come from? And why does it automatically imply $\Leftrightarrow\;\Gamma_{out}\Gamma_T=1$? (click to expand)
The high-school version: think of echoes, with no S-parameters at all

Γ = the echo factor. Shout “1” at a wall and it echoes back; $\Gamma$ is how many times your shout comes back (including the timing = phase). Shout at the tank (the left wall) → it returns $\Gamma_L$ times; shout at the transistor → it returns $\Gamma_\text{in}$ times.

The wall and the transistor face each other, and the sound bounces back and forth between them: on every round trip it is multiplied by $\Gamma_\text{in}\times\Gamma_L$.

Factor per lapWhat happens
$<1$Quieter each lap → the echo graduallydies away(what you want in an amplifier)
$>1$Louder each lap → growing without bound(the start-up phase)
$=1$ (timing exactly right)Every laprestores it exactly → the soundgoes on for ever = oscillation!

Why a negative resistance is needed:a wall is inert and at best reflects everything ($|\Gamma_L|\le1$, usually absorbing a little) → for the product to reach 1, the transistor mustshout back louder than you shouted($|\Gamma_\text{in}|>1$) — it amplifies the echo = it supplies energy = a negative resistance.

Why the right-hand condition follows automatically from the left:$\Gamma_\text{in}$ is not a property of the transistor alone — shout from the left and the echo has two parts: what bounces straight off the surface ($S_{11}$) plus what passes through the transistor → hits the right-hand wall T → bounces back → and emerges again(that is, thesecond term $\frac{S_{12}S_{21}\Gamma_T}{1-S_{22}\Gamma_T}$of $\Gamma_\text{in} = S_{11} + \frac{S_{12}S_{21}\Gamma_T}{1-S_{22}\Gamma_T}$ in the eq-box above, which is why $\Gamma_T$ appears inside it: $S_{21}$ goes in, $\Gamma_T$ reflects off the right-hand wall, $S_{12}$ comes back out, and the denominator $1-S_{22}\Gamma_T$ is the geometric series of multiple round trips between the transistor's right side and wall T). What the sound really occupies isthe whole corridor: wall L ↔ transistor ↔ wall T. “Listening at the left doorway for the echo to be restored each lap” and “listening at the right doorway” aretwo listening positions on the same everlasting echo— one performance, so of course they hold or fail together. The algebra below is just a formal check.

① $\Gamma_{in}\Gamma_L=1$ — the wave must be restored after one round trip at the reference plane. At the reference plane between the tank (the L network) and the transistor input port:

$$a_1 \xrightarrow{\;\text{into the transistor, reflect}\;} b_1=\Gamma_\text{in}a_1 \xrightarrow{\;\text{into the tank, reflect back}\;} a_1'=\Gamma_L b_1=\Gamma_L\Gamma_\text{in}\,a_1$$

With no applied sourceyet a stable finite wave → one round trip must restore it exactly, $a_1'=a_1$ ⇒ $\Gamma_\text{in}\Gamma_L=1$. This is the wave version of Barkhausen: $\Gamma_\text{in}\Gamma_L$ is the loop gain of the bouncing loop, and the condition is $=1\angle 0°$. Substituting $\Gamma=\frac{Z-Z_0}{Z+Z_0}$ and rearranging gives the one-port condition $Z_\text{in}+Z_L=0$ (the red-shaded passage above). A passive tank has $|\Gamma_L|\le 1$ ⇒ we need $|\Gamma_\text{in}|\ge 1$ ⇒ a negative resistanceof the image-reject mixer at low IF.

② $\Leftrightarrow\;\Gamma_{out}\Gamma_T=1$ — two lines of pure algebra. First put $\Gamma_\text{in}$ over a common denominator in compact form (with $\Delta=S_{11}S_{22}-S_{12}S_{21}$):

$$\Gamma_\text{in}=S_{11}+\frac{S_{12}S_{21}\Gamma_T}{1-S_{22}\Gamma_T}=\frac{S_{11}-\Delta\,\Gamma_T}{1-S_{22}\Gamma_T}$$

Substitute into $\Gamma_\text{in}\Gamma_L=1$, cross-multiply and collect the terms containing $\Gamma_T$ on the left:

$$\Gamma_L(S_{11}-\Delta\Gamma_T)=1-S_{22}\Gamma_T\;\Longrightarrow\;\Gamma_T(S_{22}-\Delta\Gamma_L)=1-S_{11}\Gamma_L\;\Longrightarrow\;\Gamma_T\cdot\underbrace{\frac{S_{22}-\Delta\Gamma_L}{1-S_{11}\Gamma_L}}_{=\;\Gamma_\text{out}}=1\;\;\blacksquare$$
Physical meaning — designing one port is enough
Oscillation is a property ofthe whole loopand belongs to no single port: if the input port satisfies the oscillation condition, the output portautomaticallydoes too. So in design (HW10) you work on one port only — pick $\Gamma_T$ → compute $\Gamma_L$ — and the condition at the other port comes free. That is the origin of Pozar's remark that “the oscillation condition of the terminating network is also satisfied”.

For an oscillator we want a transistor with a high degree of instability. The common-source or common-gate FET with a series inductor (to increase the feedback) is a standard choice.

Why do $C_T$ and $R_T$ look like that? — a three-line derivation (click to expand)

What $C_T$ and $R_T$ are:the output stability circle isa circledrawn in the $\Gamma_T$ plane (the Smith chart). $C_T$ is that circle'sThe centre is— a complex number denoting a point on the chart ($1.08\angle{33°}$ = a distance 1.08 from the origin at a bearing of 33°; note that it lies outside the unit circle); $R_T$ is itsRadius, the pure real number 0.665. Every point on the circle is a termination making $|\Gamma_\text{in}|$ exactly $= 1$; the circle therefore cuts the $\Gamma_T$ plane into two regions: $|\Gamma_\text{in}| < 1$ (stable) on one side and $|\Gamma_\text{in}| > 1$ (negative resistance) on the other.

The starting point of the derivation:the stability circle is what the boundary “$|\Gamma_\text{in}| = 1$” looks like in the $\Gamma_T$ plane. In compact form (with $\Delta' = S'_{11}S'_{22} - S'_{12}S'_{21}$):

$$\Gamma_\text{in} = \frac{S'_{11} - \Delta'\,\Gamma_T}{1 - S'_{22}\,\Gamma_T}\,,\qquad |\Gamma_\text{in}| = 1 \;\Longleftrightarrow\; \bigl|S'_{11} - \Delta'\Gamma_T\bigr| = \bigl|1 - S'_{22}\Gamma_T\bigr|$$

Expanding:Square both sides ($|a|^2 = aa^*$) and collect the $\Gamma_T$ terms:

$$\bigl(|S'_{22}|^2 - |\Delta'|^2\bigr)\,|\Gamma_T|^2 \;-\; \bigl(S'_{22} - \Delta' S_{11}'^*\bigr)\Gamma_T \;-\; \bigl(S'_{22} - \Delta' S_{11}'^*\bigr)^{\!*}\,\Gamma_T^* \;=\; |S'_{11}|^2 - 1$$

Complete the square:Divide by $D \equiv |S'_{22}|^2 - |\Delta'|^2$ to obtain the form $|\Gamma_T - C_T|^2 = R_T^2$. Comparing with $|\Gamma_T - C_T|^2 = |\Gamma_T|^2 - C_T^*\Gamma_T - C_T\Gamma_T^* + |C_T|^2$, the coefficient of $\Gamma_T$ matches $C_T^*$ —and that is where the conjugate in the formula comes from:

$$C_T = \frac{\bigl(S'_{22} - \Delta' S_{11}'^*\bigr)^*}{D}\,,\qquad R_T^2 = |C_T|^2 + \frac{|S'_{11}|^2 - 1}{D} = \frac{|S'_{12}S'_{21}|^2}{D^2}$$

The last step uses the identity $\bigl|S'_{22} - \Delta' S_{11}'^*\bigr|^2 = |S'_{12}S'_{21}|^2 + D\,(1 - |S'_{11}|^2)$ (verifiable numerically in this example: $5.64^2 \approx 3.47^2 + (-5.25)(-3.75)$ ✓), so $R_T = |S'_{12}S'_{21}/D|$.

Geometric intuition
$\Gamma_T \mapsto \Gamma_\text{in}$ is a bilinear (Möbius) transformation —circles always map to circles. $|\Gamma_\text{in}| = 1$ is the unit circle in the $\Gamma_\text{in}$ plane, so its preimage in the $\Gamma_T$ plane must also be a circle: centre $C_T$, radius $R_T$. Which side of the circle is stable is settled with a test point (the centre $\Gamma_T = 0$ gives $\Gamma_\text{in} = S'_{11}$, and $|S'_{11}| = 2.18 > 1$ → the side containing the centre is unstable). The input stability-circle formula has the same form with 1↔2 interchanged.

Since $|S'_{11}|=2.18>1$, the stable region is inside the circle. Choose $\Gamma_T$ on the unstable side, near the circle edge for maximum negative resistance.

Step 3 — Select operating point. Take $\Gamma_T = 0.59\,\angle{-104°}$ (slide note: use the impedance-locus method of Esdale & Howes, where the intersection of the two loci is the optimum $\Gamma_T$).

Step 4 — Compute required $\Gamma_L$: From the oscillation condition $\Gamma_\text{in}\Gamma_L = 1$:

$$\frac{1}{\Gamma_L} = \Gamma_\text{in} = S'_{11} + \frac{S'_{12}S'_{21}\Gamma_T}{1-S'_{22}\Gamma_T} = 3.96\,\angle{-2.4°} \;\Rightarrow\; \Gamma_L = 0.25\,\angle{2.4°}$$

Convert to impedance: $Z_L = Z_0 (1+\Gamma_L)/(1-\Gamma_L) = 84 + j1.9\,\Omega$ (equivalently $Z_\text{in} = -84 - j1.9\,\Omega$, with $Z_L = -Z_\text{in}$; the load is passive, so $R_L > 0$).

Step 5 — Empirical scaling. $84\,\Omega$ is the value that exactly balances in steady state; to let the oscillator start up, $R_L$ is actually taken as one third of the computed value (the reactance unchanged):

$$Z_L = \frac{84}{3} + j1.9 \approx 28 + j1.9 \,\Omega$$

Then at small signal $R_\text{in} + R_L = -84 + 28 = -56\,\Omega < 0$, and the net negative resistance guarantees that oscillation builds up (the $R_L = |R_\text{in}|/3$ rule of 5.1.5); as the amplitude grows $|R_\text{in}(A)|$ falls until $|R_\text{in}| = R_L$ and steady state is reached (5.1.6). In the circuit this $28 + j1.9\,\Omega$ is realized by the left-hand 90 Ω shunt resistor plus a 0.262λ 50 Ω line— transform the 90 Ω along the 0.262λ line to the input plane and there it is.

How is the Smith chart used? Walking the whole design across the chart (click to expand)

This Smith chart is drawn in the $\Gamma_T$ plane— every point on it represents a candidate terminating network. The whole design does only three things: draw the stability circle → pick $\Gamma_T$ in the unstable region → “walk” to that point on the chart using a line section and a stub.

① Draw the output stability circle. Use the $S'$ parameters to compute $C_T = 1.08\angle{33°}$ and $R_T = 0.665$ (Step 2), then draw the circle of radius $R_T$ about $C_T$. This circle is the boundary “$|\Gamma_\text{in}| = 1$”: one side gives $|\Gamma_\text{in}|<1$ (stable) and the other $|\Gamma_\text{in}|>1$ (negative resistance).

② Decide which side is stable — test the chart centre. Take $\Gamma_T = 0$ (the centre = a 50 Ω termination): then $\Gamma_\text{in} = S'_{11}$ and $|S'_{11}| = 2.18 > 1$ → the centre is on theUnstableside. The distance from the centre to $C_T$ is $= 1.08 > R_T = 0.665$ → the centre liesoutsidethe circle. Conclusion:outside the circle = unstable, inside = stable(per the slides: the stable region is inside the circle).

③ Pick $\Gamma_T$ in the unstable region. Amplifier design avoids the unstable region; oscillator design does the opposite anddeliberately pick the unstable region, and the further from the stability circle the larger $|\Gamma_\text{in}|$ and the deeper the negative resistance. Choose $\Gamma_T = 0.59\angle{-104°}$: $|\Gamma_T - C_T| = 1.56 \gg R_T$ (deep in the unstable region), and $|\Gamma_T| < 1$ so a passive network can realize it. (The basis for the choice is the Esdale & Howes impedance-locus method: plot the device locus and the load locus on the chart, andthe intersection of the two loci is the optimum operating point.)

④ Realize $\Gamma_T$ on the Smith chart — where the terminating network comes from. Starting from the 50 Ω load (= the chart centre, $\Gamma = 0$) and working towards the drain:

  • Shunt 0.346λ open stub: $y_\text{stub} = j\tan(\beta l) = j\tan 124.6° = -j1.45$, moving along the constant-conductance circle g = 1to $y = 1 - j1.45$ → $\Gamma = 0.59\angle{+126°}$.
  • Series 0.319λ 50 Ω line: $|\Gamma|$ is unchanged, so move along the $|\Gamma| = 0.59$ circletowards the generator (clockwise) by $2\beta l = 2\times0.319\times360° = 229.7°$ → $0.59\angle{(126°-229.7°)} = 0.59\angle{-104°} = \Gamma_T$ ✓ — and that is how the two component lengths were worked backwards.

⑤ $\Gamma_T \to \Gamma_\text{in}$. Substituting back into Step 4 gives $\Gamma_\text{in} = 3.96\angle{-2.4°}$ —off the edge of the Smith chart($|\Gamma|>1 \Leftrightarrow R<0$), which cannot be plotted on the chart at all — precisely the signature of an active / negative resistance; it corresponds to $Z_\text{in} = -84 - j1.9\,\Omega$.

⑥ The tuning network uses the same trick (at the input plane). The goal: use a 90 Ω shunt resistor plus a 0.262λ 50 Ω line to produce the $Z_L \approx 28 + j1.9\,\Omega$ required by Step 5 at the input reference plane. Step by step:

  • (i) Normalize and fix the starting point. The termination is a pure 90 Ω resistance → $z = 90/50 = 1.8$, lying on the right-hand half of the real axisof the Smith chart ($z>1$); $\Gamma = \dfrac{z-1}{z+1} = \dfrac{0.8}{2.8} = 0.286\angle{0°}$ (the $\Gamma$ of a purely real impedance must be real, hence an angle of 0°).
  • (ii) Rotate in phase along the line. A lossless 50 Ω line changes only the phase, not the magnitude: $|\Gamma| = 0.286$ is fixed, and we move along the constant-$|\Gamma|$ circle towards the generator (clockwise) by $2\beta l = 2\times 0.262\times 360° = 188.6°$.Why $2\beta l$: the reflected wave travels the length $l$ once out and once back, for a total phase difference of $2\beta l$.
  • (iii) The new position. $0° - 188.6° = -188.6° \equiv +171.4°$ → $\Gamma = 0.286\angle{171.4°}$ — rotated round close to theleft endof the real axis (an angle ≈ 180° = the low-impedance region).
  • (iv) Read the impedance back off. $\Gamma = -0.283 + j0.043$; $z = \dfrac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma} = \dfrac{0.717+j0.043}{1.283-j0.043} = 0.556 + j0.052$ → $Z_L = 50z \approx \mathbf{27.8 + j2.6 \approx 28 + j2\,\Omega}$ ✓, agreeing with the $28+j1.9$ of Step 5 (the small difference comes from rounding the line length and reading off the chart).
  • (v) Physical intuition. $0.262\lambda$ is only $0.012\lambda$ more than $\lambda/4$: a quarter-wave line is animpedance inverter, turning $z=1.8$ into $1/1.8 = 0.556$ (exactly the real part required!); the extra little length rotates $\Gamma$ a further $8.6°$, supplying the small $+j$ inductive part — and that is why the values 90 Ω and 0.262λ were chosen.
The role of the Smith chart in oscillator design, in one sentence
(1) Draw the stability circle to divide the $\Gamma_T$ plane into stable and unstable regions; (2) deliberately pick the unstable regionwhen choosing $\Gamma_T$ (the exact opposite of amplifier design); (3) realize the chosen $\Gamma_T$ and $Z_L$ on the chart using the two kinds of move — “along a constant-conductance circle (a shunt stub) and along a constant-$|\Gamma|$ circle (a series line)” — and read off the electrical lengths of the stub and the line.

5.1.8 Oscillator Phase Noise — Definition

The output of a real oscillator is not a perfect sinusoid but carries small amplitude and phase fluctuations:

$$v_o(t) = V_0\bigl[1 + r(t)\bigr]\cos\bigl[\omega_0 t + \theta(t)\bigr]$$
  • $r(t)$ — amplitude fluctuation (typically well-controlled by limiting / AGC)
  • $\theta(t)$ — phase fluctuation (discrete spurs from mixer products and harmonics, OR random from flicker/thermal noise). The instantaneous phase variation looks the same as an instantaneous frequency variation.

Derivation of $\mathcal{L}(\omega)$

Assume small phase modulation: $r(t)\approx 0$ and $\theta(t) = \theta_p\sin(\Delta\omega\, t)$ ($\Delta\omega$ = the frequency offset, $\theta_p \ll 1$ rad). Expanding step by step:

$$v_o(t) = V_0\cos\bigl[\omega_0 t + \theta(t)\bigr]$$ $$= V_0\cos\omega_0 t\,\cos\theta(t) - V_0\sin\omega_0 t\,\sin\theta(t)$$
← the angle-addition formula $\cos(A+B)=\cos A\cos B-\sin A\sin B$ with $A=\omega_0 t$ and $B=\theta(t)$
$$\approx V_0\cos\omega_0 t - V_0\sin\omega_0 t\cdot(\theta_p\sin\Delta\omega\, t)$$
← the small-angle approximation: $\theta_p\ll 1$ ⇒ $\cos\theta\approx 1$ and $\sin\theta\approx\theta = \theta_p\sin\Delta\omega\, t$
$$= V_0\cos\omega_0 t - \frac{V_0\theta_p}{2}\bigl[\cos(\omega_0-\Delta\omega)t - \cos(\omega_0+\Delta\omega)t\bigr]$$
← the product-to-sum identity $\sin A\sin B=\tfrac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B)]$

Reading off the physics:the carrier $V_0\cos\omega_0 t$ stays put and beside it growa pair of mirror-image sidebands at $\omega_0\pm\Delta\omega$, each of amplitude $V_0\theta_p/2$ — precisely the skirt and spurs hanging symmetrically about the carrier in the figure above (caused by phase noise); as $\theta(t)$ sweeps over all $\Delta\omega$,the discrete pairs of sidebands smear into a continuous noise skirt. Each sideband at $\omega_0\pm\Delta\omega$ has power $\left(V_0\theta_p/2\right)^2/2 = V_0^2\theta_p^2/8$. Normalizing to carrier power $V_0^2/2$:

Single-sideband phase noise $$\mathcal{L}(\Delta\omega) \;=\; \frac{\text{SSB noise power per Hz at }\Delta\omega}{\text{Carrier power}} \;=\; \frac{\bigl(V_0\theta_p/2\bigr)^2}{V_0^2} \;=\; \frac{\theta_p^2}{4} \;=\; \frac{\theta^2_{rms}}{2}\;\;\text{(1/Hz)}$$
the sideband power $(V_0\theta_p/2)^2/2$ ÷ the carrier power $V_0^2/2$; the two factors of $1/2$ cancel, leaving only $V_0^2$ in the denominator

Expressed in dBc/Hz (decibels relative to carrier per hertz):

$$\mathcal{L}(\Delta\omega) \;\big|_\text{dBc/Hz} = 10\log_{10}\left(\frac{\theta^2_{\text{rms}}}{2}\right)$$

5.1.9 Leeson's Model for Oscillator Phase Noise

Leeson treats the oscillator as a noisy unity-gain feedback amplifier with a high-$Q$ tank as the band-limiting element. The model predicts the offset-frequency behavior of $\mathcal{L}(\Delta\omega)$.

Building blocks

  1. Input noise PSD (thermal + flicker):

    The thermal noise floor is white at $kT_0/2$; flicker noise varies as $1/\omega$, and its PSD is generally written (per the slides):

    $$S_f(\Delta\omega) = \frac{kT_0}{2}\cdot\frac{\omega_\alpha}{\Delta\omega}$$
    $\omega_\alpha$ ismeasured empirically(set by the process and the device; it cannot be computed from theory); the PSD isinversely proportionalto the frequency offset (per the slide note)

    Add the two, include the amplifier noise figure $F$, and divide by $P_0$ to normalize relative to the carrier:

    $$S(\Delta\omega) = \frac{kT_0}{2}\left(1 + \frac{\omega_\alpha}{\Delta\omega}\right)F/P_0 \quad(\text{1/Hz})$$

5.1.10 Receiver Phase-Noise Requirement (Reciprocal Mixing)

Phase noise on the receiver's local oscillator degrades both SNR/BER and selectivity. The mechanism is called reciprocal mixing:

Reciprocal mixing
A nearby adjacent-channel interferer at $f_0 + \Delta f$ mixes with the LO phase-noise skirt at offset $\Delta f$, landing in the IF passband as in-band noise. The LO's $\mathcal{L}(\Delta f)$ at the offset matching the interferer determines how much energy gets translated in.

Phase-noise spec derivation

Let:

  • $C$ = desired carrier power (dBm)
  • $I$ = interferer power (dBm) at offset $\Delta\omega$
  • $B$ = IF bandwidth (Hz)
  • $S$ = required adjacent-channel rejection / SIR (dB)

The desired signal is downconverted by the LO carrier(conversion gain $G_{c,c}$); an interferer, meanwhile, is downconverted by riding on the LO'sphase-noise sidebandat offset $\Delta\omega$ (conversion gain $G_{c,n}$). The signal-to-interference ratio at IF is:

$$S=\bbox[#fff3b0,4px]{\frac{\text{IF signal power}}{\text{IF noise power}}}=\frac{|G_{c,c}|^2\cdot C}{|G_{c,n}|^2\cdot I}=\frac{P_0\cdot C}{P_n\cdot I}=\frac{P_0\cdot C}{\big(P_0\cdot\mathcal{L}(\Delta\omega)\cdot B\big)\cdot I}=\frac{C}{\mathcal{L}(\Delta\omega)\cdot B\cdot I}$$ $$\Rightarrow\; \mathcal{L}(\Delta\omega) = \frac{C}{S\cdot I\cdot B}\;\;\text{(1/Hz)}$$
Working through the chain of equalities step by step (click to expand)
  • The second equality:the conversion (power) gain is proportional to “the LO power at that frequency” → the carrier path has $|G_{c,c}|^2 \propto P_0$ (the LO oscillation power) and the noise path $|G_{c,n}|^2 \propto P_n$ (the sideband power at offset $\Delta\omega$).
  • The third equality:by the definition of $\mathcal{L}$, the sideband power = the carrier power × the phase noise × the bandwidth: $P_n = P_0\cdot\mathcal{L}(\Delta\omega)\cdot B$ ($B$ = the IF bandwidth, integrating the per-Hz $\mathcal{L}$ across the whole channel).
  • The fourth equality:$P_0$ cancels between numerator and denominatorcancels → the result is independent of LO power, leaving only $C$, $I$, $\mathcal{L}$ and $B$ — so raising the LO power does nothing for reciprocal mixing; the only cure is to lower $\mathcal{L}$.

In dBc/Hz:

LO phase-noise spec from receiver requirements $$\boxed{\;\mathcal{L}(\Delta\omega) \le C_\text{dBm} - S_\text{dB} - I_\text{dBm} - 10\log_{10}B\;}$$

5.2 Frequency Synthesis Methods

What a frequency synthesizer is
A frequency synthesizer derives a large number of precisely-controlled output frequencies from a single stable reference oscillator — usually a crystal-controlled oscillator, sometimes housed in a temperature-controlled oven (OCXO) for extreme stability (≤0.01 ppm).

Three basic synthesis methods exist:

  1. Direct synthesis — analog mixing, multiplication, division, filtering (oldest method).
  2. Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) — feedback control around a VCO that follows a divided reference.
  3. Direct Digital Synthesis (DDS) — digital phase accumulator + sine lookup + DAC (newest).

5.2.1 Direct Synthesis

Direct frequency synthesis is an analog method using a chain of mixers, frequency multipliers (×N), dividers (÷M), bandpass filters, and switches. The fundamental source is usually a temperature-controlled crystal oscillator (TCXO) for stable output and excellent phase noise.

Pros: excellent phase noise (low-noise multipliers/dividers), no settling time after a switch.

Cons: complex and expensive when wide coverage with fine resolution is needed → mostly reserved for RF/microwave test instruments (signal generators, network analyzers).

5.2.2 Phase-Locked Loops (PLL)

A PLL uses a feedback control circuit to make a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) precisely track the phase of a stable reference oscillator. Its key feature: the VCO output can be made to run at an integer multiple of the reference, by inserting a digital divider $\div N$ in the feedback path.

When the loop is locked: $f_\text{PFD,1} = f_\text{PFD,2}$, i.e., $f_\text{ref} = f_\text{out}/N$, giving:

$$\boxed{\;f_\text{out} = N\cdot f_\text{ref}\;}$$

Applications

  • Frequency synthesizers (cellular, WiFi, GPS)
  • FM demodulators (PLL acts as a frequency-to-voltage converter)
  • Carrier recovery circuits in coherent receivers
  • Clock-and-data recovery in digital communication links

Key trade-offs of integer-N PLL

  • Excellent frequency accuracy and phase noise (inherits reference stability inside loop BW; VCO noise dominates outside loop BW).
  • Step size = $f_\text{ref}$: output frequency is constrained to integer multiples of the reference.
  • Trade-off: to get a small step size, $f_\text{ref}$ must be small, which means $N$ must be large; this gives long settling time (loop BW ≪ $f_\text{ref}$) and noisier output (the in-band noise scales as $20\log N$).
The integer-N step-size problem
For a 900 MHz output with 200 kHz channel spacing (GSM), you need $f_\text{ref} = 200\,\text{kHz}$ and $N = 4500$. The loop BW must be ≪ 200 kHz (say 10–20 kHz), giving slow settling (~100 μs) and elevated in-band noise (+$20\log 4500\approx +73\,\text{dB}$ above ref-source noise floor!). This drives the move to fractional-N.

5.2.3 Fractional-N Phase-Locked Loops

A fractional-N PLL provides a non-integer-valued division ratio, so the VCO output can be at an integer-plus-fractional multiple of the reference. This decouples the step size from the reference frequency.

How it works

A digital divider can only divide by aninteger, but a non-integer divisor between $N$ and $N+1$ can be approximated byswitching the divider dynamically:

  • Of every $R$ reference cycles, $K$ divide by $(N+1)$.
  • The remaining $(R-K)$ cycles divide by $N$.

The average division ratio is:

$$N_\text{avg} = \frac{(R-K)\cdot N + K\cdot(N+1)}{R} = N + \frac{K}{R}$$
The numerator = thetotal number of VCO cyclesin $R$ reference cycles: the $(R-K)$ cycles set to $\div N$ each accumulate $N$ cycles (the first term) plus the $K$ cycles switched to $\div(N{+}1)$, each accumulating $N{+}1$ (the second term); divide by $R$ to get the average divisor

So the output frequency is:

Fractional-N output $$\boxed{\;f_\text{out} = \left(N + \frac{K}{R}\right)f_\text{ref}\;}$$
The slides give the special case $K=1$ (only 1 of the $R$ cycles divides by $N{+}1$): $f_\text{out} = \frac{N(R-1)+(N+1)}{R}f_0 = \bigl(N+\frac{1}{R}\bigr)f_0$ — replace the 1 with a general $K$ and you have the expression above; the slides' $f_0$ is the $f_\text{ref}$ here

Resolution: $\Delta f = f_\text{ref}/R$, which can be made very fine (sub-Hz with large $R$) without reducing $f_\text{ref}$.

Why this matters
With $f_\text{ref}=$ 10 MHz and $R=10000$, you can get $\Delta f = 1\,\text{kHz}$ resolution while keeping the PLL loop BW at ~100 kHz (fast settling, low close-in noise). Modern Σ-Δ fractional-N synthesizers use multi-bit pseudo-random divider modulation to push spurs (the byproduct of toggling) into the noise-shaped high-frequency region.

Drawback: fractional spurs

The periodic toggling between $N$ and $N+1$ produces a sawtooth phase-error pattern that creates fractional spurs at $f_\text{ref}/R$ and harmonics. Σ-Δ modulation of the divider randomizes the pattern, suppressing these spurs and shaping the residual quantization noise to high offsets where the loop filter rejects it.

5.2.4 Direct Digital Synthesis (DDS)

DDS uses a digital phase accumulator to step through a sine look-up table at a programmable rate, then converts the digital sine samples to analog via a high-speed DAC and a reconstruction LPF.

The output frequency is set entirely digitally by the Frequency Tuning Word (FTW):

DDS output frequency $$\boxed{\;f_\text{out} = \frac{\text{FTW}}{2^M}\,f_\text{clk}\;}$$

where $M$ is the accumulator width in bits and $f_\text{clk}$ is the clock rate.

Strengths

  • Extremely fine resolution — sub-Hz with a 32- or 48-bit accumulator.
  • Instantaneous frequency switching — change FTW between clock cycles, no settling.
  • Phase-continuous hopping for chirps, FSK, FM.
  • Frequency, phase, amplitude all digitally controlled.

Limitations

  • Maximum output frequency limited by DAC speed: $f_\text{out,max} \approx 0.4 f_\text{clk}$ (Nyquist with image rejection).
  • Spurs from DAC nonlinearity and LUT phase truncation.
  • Quantization noise floor limits SFDR.

Modern DDS chips (e.g., Analog Devices AD9914) can clock at 3.5 GS/s for output up to ~1.4 GHz.

Practice 2 — Leeson scaling

Problem. A 1 GHz oscillator has phase noise $-100\,\text{dBc/Hz}$ at 10 kHz offset in the $1/f^2$ region. If the resonator Q is doubled (everything else equal), what is the new phase noise?

Solution

Given / required: a 1 GHz oscillator with $\mathcal{L}(10\,\text{kHz}) = -100\,\text{dBc/Hz}$ in the $1/f^2$ region; Q is doubled with everything else unchanged. Find the new phase noise.

Formulas and sources(the Leeson model, 5.1.9, $1/f^2$ region):

$$\mathcal{L}(f_m) = \frac{FkT_0}{2P_0}\left(\frac{f_0}{2Qf_m}\right)^2 \;\propto\; \frac{1}{Q^2}$$

Q is squared inside the bracketed denominator — the most important scaling relation in the Leeson model.

Substituting step by step: $Q \to 2Q$:

$$\frac{\mathcal{L}_\text{new}}{\mathcal{L}_\text{old}} = \left(\frac{Q_\text{old}}{2Q_\text{old}}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4} \;\Rightarrow\; 10\log\frac{1}{4} = -6.0\,\text{dB}$$

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{new} = -100 - 6 = \mathbf{-106\,dBc/Hz}\ @\ 10\,\text{kHz}$$

Physical interpretation: A mnemonic: in the $1/f^2$ region, “double Q = −6 dB; double the power = −3 dB; double the offset = −6 dB”. Note that this scaling holds only in the $1/f^2$ region — once you reach the noise floor ($f_m > f_0/2Q$) no amount of Q will push $FkT_0/2P_0$ down; and doubling Q also pulls the $1/f^2$ region in towards lower offsets, so the whole skirt moves inwards.

Solution

Given / required: $C = -85\,\text{dBm}$, $I = -25\,\text{dBm}$ @ $\Delta f = 1\,\text{MHz}$, $S = 12\,\text{dB}$, $B = 30\,\text{kHz}$. Find the upper limit on the LO phase noise at 1 MHz offset.

$$\mathcal{L} \leq -85 - 12 - (-25) - 44.77 = -85 - 12 + 25 - 44.77 = \mathbf{-116.8\,dBc/Hz}\ @\ 1\,\text{MHz}$$

Problem. A fractional-N PLL has $f_\text{ref} = 26\,\text{MHz}$ and uses a 20-bit fractional divider. What is the minimum frequency step?

Specification interpretation — ppm/°C

Substituting step by step:

(b) Recommend an oscillator architecture (free-running LC vs crystal-PLL vs TCXO) and justify.

(c) The phase-noise spec at 100 kHz offset implies what minimum loaded $Q$ if Leeson's $1/f^2$ term dominates? Use $\mathcal{L} = (kT_0F/2P_0)(\omega_0/2Q\omega_m)^2$, with $F = 6\,\text{dB}$, $P_0 = -3\,\text{dBm}$ (oscillator core).

Answer

Given / required: $f_0 = 915\,\text{MHz}$, a frequency accuracy of ±25 kHz over $-40..+85$ °C, phase noise ≤ −95 dBc/Hz @ 100 kHz, $F = 6\,\text{dB}$ and $P_0 = -3\,\text{dBm}$. Find (a) the temperature-stability requirement, (b) the architecture choice, and (c) the minimum loaded Q from Leeson.

(a) Temperature stability(unit conversion):

The temperature span is $\Delta T = 85 - (-40) = 125$ °C.

The permissible relative offset: $\dfrac{\pm 25\,\text{kHz}}{915\,\text{MHz}} = \dfrac{2.5\times 10^4}{9.15\times 10^8} = 2.73\times 10^{-5} = \pm 27.3\,\text{ppm}$.

Spread over the whole temperature range: $\dfrac{27.3\,\text{ppm}}{125\,\text{°C}} \approx \mathbf{0.22\,ppm/°C}$.

(b) Architecture choice: 0.22 ppm/°C is far beyond a free-running LC (typically ~100 ppm/°C, 500× worse) and is tight even for a cheap crystal (of the order of 1–10 ppm/°C including ageing). The recommendation is a crystal-referenced PLL:26 MHz TCXO reference (±0.5 ppm over temperature is standard commercial grade, corresponding to ±458 Hz at 915 MHz ≪ 25 kHz) plus an integer-N or fractional-N divider (5.2.2/5.2.3). The PLL multiplies up the TCXO's stability while the VCO's own drift is held by the loop; integration is high and the current can be under 3 mA (this is exactly the architecture of a modern sub-GHz transceiver SoC).

(c) Deriving Q backwards from Leeson(the Leeson model 5.1.9, $1/f^2$ region):

$$\mathcal{L}(f_m) = \frac{kT_0 F}{2P_0}\left(\frac{\omega_0}{2Q\,\omega_m}\right)^2$$

Unit conversion (dB → linear):

  • $\mathcal{L} \leq -95\,\text{dBc/Hz} \Rightarrow 10^{-95/10} = 10^{-9.5} = 3.16\times 10^{-10}\,/\text{Hz}$
  • $F = 6\,\text{dB} \Rightarrow 10^{6/10} = 3.98 \approx 4$
  • $P_0 = -3\,\text{dBm} \Rightarrow 10^{-3/10}\,\text{mW} = 0.5\,\text{mW} = 5\times 10^{-4}\,\text{W}$
  • $kT_0 = 1.38\times 10^{-23}\times 290 = 4\times 10^{-21}$ W·s

The noise prefactor: $\dfrac{kT_0 F}{2P_0} = \dfrac{4\times10^{-21}\times 4}{2\times 5\times 10^{-4}} = \dfrac{1.6\times 10^{-20}}{10^{-3}} = 1.6\times 10^{-17}\,/\text{Hz}$.

Solving for the resonant amplification term: $\left(\dfrac{\omega_0}{2Q\omega_m}\right)^2 \leq \dfrac{\mathcal{L}}{kT_0F/2P_0} = \dfrac{3.16\times 10^{-10}}{1.6\times 10^{-17}} = 1.97\times 10^7$.

Taking the square root: $\dfrac{\omega_0}{2Q\omega_m} \leq \sqrt{1.97\times 10^7} = 4441$ (note that $\omega_0/\omega_m = f_0/f_m$, the 2π cancelling).

$$Q \geq \frac{f_0}{2 f_m \cdot 4441} = \frac{915\times 10^6}{2\times 10^5 \times 4441} = \frac{915\times 10^6}{8.88\times 10^8} = \mathbf{1.03}$$

A loaded Q of only ~1 is needed for −95 dBc/Hz @ 100 kHz — easily met by a typical integrated LC tank (Q ≈ 8–15).

Physical interpretation: This problem shows that the bottleneck at 915 MHz isfrequency accuracy(which forces the PLL architecture) rather than phase noise (Q ≈ 1 would do, an easy margin of more than tenfold). −95 dBc/Hz @ 100 kHz is a relaxed specification for sub-GHz ISM; for cellular (of the order of −118, see P5.2) the Q requirement rises immediately.

2.1 Easy
Colpitts frequency & gain ratio

A common-emitter Colpitts oscillator uses $L_3 = 1.0\,\mu\text{H}$, $C_1 = 220\,\text{pF}$, $C_2 = 470\,\text{pF}$.

(a) Find $\omega_0$ and $f_0$.

(b) What ratio $g_m/G_i$ is required?

Answer

Given / required: a CE Colpitts with $L_3 = 1.0\,\mu\text{H} = 10^{-6}\,\text{H}$, $C_1 = 220\,\text{pF}$, $C_2 = 470\,\text{pF}$ and $G_i = 1/10\,\text{k}\Omega = 10^{-4}\,\text{S}$. Find $\omega_0$, $f_0$, $g_m/G_i$ and the minimum $g_m$.

Formulas and sources(the three-reactance oscillation condition $X_1+X_2+X_3=0$, 5.1.4): in Colpitts the two capacitors in series resonate with $L_3$,

$$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L_3\,C_\text{eq}}},\quad C_\text{eq} = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1+C_2},\qquad \frac{g_m}{G_i} \geq \frac{C_2}{C_1}\ \text{(the Barkhausen amplitude condition)}$$

Substituting step by step:

(a) $C_\text{eq} = \dfrac{220\times 470}{220+470} = \dfrac{103400}{690} = 149.9\,\text{pF} = 1.499\times 10^{-10}\,\text{F}$.

$L_3 C_\text{eq} = 10^{-6} \times 1.499\times 10^{-10} = 1.499\times 10^{-16}\,\text{s}^2$, so $\sqrt{\cdot} = 1.224\times 10^{-8}\,\text{s}$.

$\omega_0 = \dfrac{1}{1.224\times 10^{-8}} = \mathbf{8.17\times 10^7\ rad/s}$.

f₀ = ω₀/(2π) = 8.17×10⁷/6.283 ≈ 1.30×10⁷ Hz ≈ 13.0 MHz

(b) The Colpitts gain condition comes from the feedback divider ratio: $\dfrac{g_m}{G_i} \geq \dfrac{C_2}{C_1} = \dfrac{470}{220} = \mathbf{2.14}$.

(c) $g_m \geq 2.14\times G_i = 2.14\times 10^{-4}\,\text{S} = \mathbf{0.214\,mS}$.

Physical interpretation: 0.214 mS is a very small $g_m$ — a BJT meets it at a collector current of only about $5.5\,\mu\text{A}$ ($g_m = I_C/V_T$), so in practice what limits start-up is not the transistor but the part of the tank loss not included in $G_i$. Do not make $C_2/C_1$ too large: a large ratio makes the phase condition easy to satisfy but weakens the feedback, demands a higher $g_m$, and distributes the output swing poorly.

2.2 Easy
Hartley frequency

A Hartley oscillator uses a tapped inductor with $L_1 = 4.7\,\mu\text{H}$, $L_2 = 22\,\mu\text{H}$, and $C_3 = 47\,\text{pF}$.

(a) Find $f_0$. (b) What is the required $g_m/G_i$?

Answer

Given / required: a Hartley oscillator with a tapped inductor $L_1 = 4.7\,\mu\text{H}$, $L_2 = 22\,\mu\text{H}$ and $C_3 = 47\,\text{pF}$. Find $f_0$ and $g_m/G_i$.

Formulas and sources(the three-reactance condition $X_1+X_2+X_3=0$, 5.1.4): Hartley uses two inductors ($X_1, X_2 > 0$) with one capacitor ($X_3 < 0$), and the two inductancesadd in series(ignoring mutual inductance) to resonate with $C_3$:

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(L_1+L_2)\,C_3}},\qquad \frac{g_m}{G_i} \geq \frac{L_1}{L_2}$$

Substituting step by step:

(a) $L_1+L_2 = 4.7 + 22 = 26.7\,\mu\text{H} = 2.67\times 10^{-5}\,\text{H}$.

$(L_1+L_2)C_3 = 2.67\times 10^{-5}\times 47\times 10^{-12} = 1.255\times 10^{-15}\,\text{s}^2$.

$\sqrt{1.255\times 10^{-15}} = 3.54\times 10^{-8}\,\text{s}$, and $2\pi\times 3.54\times 10^{-8} = 2.226\times 10^{-7}\,\text{s}$.

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2.226\times 10^{-7}} = 4.49\times 10^6\,\text{Hz} = \mathbf{4.49\,MHz}$$

(b) $\dfrac{g_m}{G_i} = \dfrac{L_1}{L_2} = \dfrac{4.7}{22} = \mathbf{0.214}$.

f₀ = 4.49 MHz; g_m/G_i = L₁/L₂ = 0.214. Note: Hartley's ratio uses inductors (L₁/L₂), opposite role from Colpitts (C₂/C₁).

Physical interpretation: Here $L_1/L_2 < 1$, so the gain condition is even more relaxed than the Colpitts of P2.1 — the tap position sets the amount of feedback. A way to remember it: in both topologies the ratio is “the reactance of the component at the input / the reactance of the component at the output”; in Colpitts the capacitive reactance $\propto 1/C$, which inverts numerator and denominator into $C_2/C_1$. Hartley is common in the MHz band, but two inductors are bulky and their mutual coupling is hard to control, so GHz integrated circuits use the Colpitts family almost exclusively.

2.3 Medium
Topology comparison: Colpitts vs Pierce for crystal use

A 32.768 kHz watch crystal (Q ≈ 50,000) is to be used in either a Colpitts or a Pierce oscillator.

(a) Which topology naturally accepts the crystal as the inductive element ($X_3$)?

(b) The crystal's series resistance is 50 kΩ. If $C_1 = C_2 = 12\,\text{pF}$, estimate the loaded $Q$ of the resonator.

(c) What is the dominant advantage of the crystal over a discrete LC for this application?

Answer

Given / required: a 32.768 kHz watch crystal with unloaded $Q_u \approx 50{,}000$, series resistance $R_s = 50\,\text{k}\Omega$ and $C_1 = C_2 = 12\,\text{pF}$. Identify the topology, estimate the loaded Q, and explain the crystal's advantages.

(a) Choice of topology(5.1.4):Pierce — it is simply a CE Colpitts with $L_3$ replaced by a crystal. A crystal isinductiveonly in the narrow interval between its series resonance $f_s$ and its parallel resonance $f_p$ ($f_p - f_s \approx f_s\,C_m/2C_0$, usually only a few hundred ppm wide), where it plays exactly the role of $X_3 > 0$; the oscillation frequency is pinned inside that narrow interval, which is the root of the crystal oscillator's stability.

(b) Estimating the loaded Q: Seen from the crystal, the two divider capacitors are in series:

$$C_L = \frac{C_1 C_2}{C_1+C_2} = \frac{12\times 12}{24} = 6\,\text{pF}$$

Estimate the loaded Q from “the ratio of $C_L$'s reactance to $R_s$”: $\omega_0 = 2\pi\times 32768 = 2.06\times 10^5$ rad/s and $X_{C_L} = 1/(\omega_0 C_L) = 1/(2.06\times 10^5 \times 6\times 10^{-12}) = 809\,\text{k}\Omega$.

$$Q_L = \frac{1}{\omega_0 R_\text{eff} C_L} \approx \mathbf{16{,}200}\quad(\text{corresponding to } R_\text{eff} = 50\,\Omega)$$

Erratum:substituting the $R_s = 50\,\text{k}\Omega$ given in the problem directly into this expression gives $809\text{k}/50\text{k} = 16.2$, a factor of 1000 away from the original answer of 16,200 — the original expression implicitly assumes $R_\text{eff} = 50\,\Omega$. The physically correct picture is this: the crystal's motional inductance is enormous ($L_m = Q_u R_s/\omega_0 \approx 12{,}000\,\text{H}$!), and the near-lossless $C_L$ only shifts the frequency without appreciably lowering Q; the real Q degradation comes from the amplifier's equivalent resistive loading. Typically the loaded Q ends up between 1/3 and 1/2 of $Q_u$, i.e. of the order of ~16,000–25,000, consistent with 16,200.

(c) Crystal vs discrete LC:

  • Frequency stability: a crystal is ≈ 10 ppm over temperature; an LC is ≈ 10,000 ppm (1%) → an improvement of ~10³.
  • Q: a crystal is ≈ 50,000; an LC is ≈ 50–200 → an improvement of ~10²–10³; and by Leeson ($\mathcal{L}\propto 1/Q^2$, 5.1.9) the phase noise improves by 40–60 dB.
The Pierce topology; loaded Q ~ 1.6×10⁴ (still over a hundred times an LC); stability and phase noise both improve by roughly three orders of magnitude — which is why a watch loses only a few seconds a month.

Physical interpretation: The price of a high Q is that it isnot tunable: a crystal can only be “pulled” a few hundred ppm between $f_s$ and $f_p$ (trimmed with $C_L$). So system design always pairs “a crystal providing a stable low-frequency reference” with “a PLL/VCO providing the tunable RF” (5.2).

Answer

Given / required: at the steady-state operating point ($I_0, \omega_0$) the device impedance is $Z_\text{in} = -30 + j40\,\Omega$. Find the $Z_L$ needed to sustain oscillation and identify its reactive character.

Formulas and sources(the one-port negative-resistance oscillation condition, 5.1.5): the total loop impedance is zero,

$$Z_\text{in}(I_0,\omega_0) + Z_L(\omega_0) = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \begin{cases} R_L = -R_\text{in} \\ X_L = -X_\text{in} \end{cases}$$

The real-part condition = amplitude balance (the power produced by the device is exactly dissipated in the load); the imaginary-part condition = frequency selection (the frequency at which the total reactance vanishes is $\omega_0$).

Substituting step by step:

(a) $R_L = -(-30) = +30\,\Omega$;$X_L = -(+40) = -40\,\Omega$:

$$Z_L = \mathbf{30 - j40\,\Omega}$$

(b) $X_L = -40\,\Omega < 0$ → capacitive. The equivalent capacitance is $C = \dfrac{1}{\omega_0 |X_L|} = \dfrac{1}{40\,\omega_0}$ (for example $C \approx 0.80\,\text{pF}$ at $f_0 = 5\,\text{GHz}$). The device's $+j40$ is inductive and the load cancels it with an equal capacitive reactance — together they form a resonator.

Physical interpretation: Note that this is thesteady-statecondition ($I_0$ being the large-signal operating point). 3.2 Medium

Answer

Given / required: $\Gamma_\text{in} = 2.5\,\angle{-30°}$ (with $Z_0 = 50\,\Omega$). Find the $\Gamma_L$ satisfying the oscillation condition, the corresponding $Z_L$, and identify where it lies.

Formulas and sources(the reflection-coefficient form of the oscillation condition, 5.1.5/5.1.6): $Z_\text{in}+Z_L = 0$ is equivalent to

$$\Gamma_L\,\Gamma_\text{in} = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; \Gamma_L = \frac{1}{\Gamma_\text{in}} \;\Rightarrow\; |\Gamma_L| = \frac{1}{|\Gamma_\text{in}|},\quad \angle\Gamma_L = -\angle\Gamma_\text{in}$$

Substituting step by step:

(a) Take the reciprocal of the magnitude and negate the phase:

$$\Gamma_L = \frac{1}{2.5}\,\angle{+30°} = \mathbf{0.4\,\angle{+30°}}$$

(b) First convert to rectangular form: $\Gamma_L = 0.4(\cos 30° + j\sin 30°) = 0.4(0.866 + j0.500) = 0.346 + j0.200$.

Substitute into $Z_L = Z_0\dfrac{1+\Gamma_L}{1-\Gamma_L}$, multiplying numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator:

$Z_L = 50\,\dfrac{1.346+j0.200}{0.654-j0.200} = 50\,\dfrac{(1.346+j0.200)(0.654+j0.200)}{0.654^2+0.200^2}$

$$Z_L = 50\,(1.795 + j0.854) = \mathbf{89.7 + j42.7\,\Omega}$$

(c) $|\Gamma_L| = 0.4 < 1$ → inside Smith chart → $R_L = 89.7\,\Omega > 0$, so it is apassive load ✓. Conversely $|\Gamma_\text{in}| = 2.5 > 1$ falls outside the Smith chart, corresponding to $R_\text{in} < 0$ (active, producing power).

Γ_L = 0.4∠+30°, Z_L = 89.7 + j42.7 Ω (passive ✓). The geometric meaning of Γ_L Γ_in = 1: the further the device sticks out beyond the chart, the closer in the conjugate direction the load must sit inside it.

Check: $Z_\text{in} = Z_0(1+\Gamma_\text{in})/(1-\Gamma_\text{in}) \approx -89.7 - j42.7\,\Omega = -Z_L$ ✓.

3.3 Design

(a) Verify the transistor is potentially unstable. (Check $|S'_{11}|>1$ or compute K.)

(b) Compute the output-port stability circle parameters $(C_T, R_T)$.

(c) For a chosen $\Gamma_T = 0.59\,\angle{-104°}$, find $\Gamma_L$ and $Z_L$ needed for oscillation.

Answer

Given / required: the 4 GHz S-parameters of a common-gate GaAs MESFET with a 5 nH series gate inductor (deliberately enhancing the instability) are as given. $$K = \frac{1-|S'_{11}|^2-|S'_{22}|^2+|\Delta'|^2}{2|S'_{12}S'_{21}|} = \frac{1-4.752-0.270+5.518}{2\times 3.47} = \frac{1.496}{6.94} = 0.22 < 1 \;\checkmark$$

C_T ≈ 1.07 ∠33°, R_T ≈ 0.66 (Pozar's textbook gives 1.08 ∠33°, 0.665 — agreement within rounding).

Deciding which side is the unstable region: $|S'_{11}| > 1$ means that at $\Gamma_T = 0$ (the chart centre) $|\Gamma_\text{in}| > 1$ → the centre is already on the unstable side; so when choosing $\Gamma_T$, staying outside the circle near the centre keeps it unstable, and the larger $|\Gamma_\text{in}|$ the better.

(c) From $\Gamma_T$ find $\Gamma_\text{in}$, $\Gamma_L$ and $Z_L$:Choose $\Gamma_T = 0.59\,\angle{-104°} = -0.143 - j0.572$ (in the inductive region, giving a large $|\Gamma_\text{in}|$).

$$\Gamma_\text{in} = S'_{11} + \frac{S'_{12}S'_{21}\Gamma_T}{1-S'_{22}\Gamma_T}$$

Denominator: $S'_{22}\Gamma_T = 0.52\times 0.59\,\angle{(155-104)°} = 0.307\,\angle{51°} = 0.193 + j0.239$;

$1 - S'_{22}\Gamma_T = 0.807 - j0.239 = 0.842\,\angle{-16.5°}$.

The second term of the numerator: $S'_{12}S'_{21}\Gamma_T = 3.47\times 0.59\,\angle{(114-104)°} = 2.047\,\angle{10°}$.

Dividing: $\dfrac{2.047\,\angle{10°}}{0.842\,\angle{-16.5°}} = 2.431\,\angle{26.5°} = 2.175 + j1.085$.

Adding $S'_{11} = 2.18\,\angle{-35°} = 1.786 - j1.250$:

$$\Gamma_\text{in} = (1.786+2.175) + j(-1.250+1.085) = 3.961 - j0.165 = \mathbf{3.96\,\angle{-2.4°}}\ \text{(matches Pozar/slides)}$$

The oscillation condition $\Gamma_L\Gamma_\text{in} = 1$: take the reciprocal of the magnitude and negate the phase:

$$\Gamma_L = \frac{1}{\Gamma_\text{in}} = \frac{1}{3.96}\,\angle{+2.4°} = \mathbf{0.25\,\angle{+2.4°}}$$

Converting to impedance: $Z_L = 50\dfrac{1+\Gamma_L}{1-\Gamma_L} = \mathbf{84 + j1.9\,\Omega}$ — passive ($R_L > 0$), as a load must be; equivalently $Z_\text{in} = -84 - j1.9\,\Omega$ and $Z_L = -Z_\text{in}$.

Start-up(empirical rule):The $84\,\Omega$ above is the “exactly balanced” value — under large signal $|R_\text{in}|$ shrinks, so taking 84 Ω from the outset might never start. The rule of thumb is to take $R_L = |R_\text{in}|/3$ leaving $X_L$ unchanged:

$$Z_L \approx \frac{84}{3} + j1.9 = \mathbf{28 + j1.9\,\Omega}$$

The small-signal net resistance is $= -84 + 28 = -56\,\Omega < 0$ → the amplitude grows exponentially until $|R_\text{in}(A)|$ falls to 28 Ω.

Physical interpretation: Each of the three steps corresponds to a physical action: the gate inductor “pushes” the device out of the stable region (creating negative resistance); the choice of $\Gamma_T$ sets how deep that negative resistance is ($|\Gamma_\text{in}| = 3.96$ is very deep, making the design robust); and the $/3$ rule trades a little output power for reliable start-up across temperature and bias. The actual oscillation frequency will differ slightly from 4 GHz (since $X_\text{in}$ also moves under large signal), with the steady-state point set by the Kurokawa condition (5.1.6).

Topic 4Phase Noise (Leeson's Model)

Four-region noise spectrum, Q dependence, flicker corner, design trade-offs.

4.1 Easy
Identify slope regions in Leeson plot

A measured oscillator phase-noise plot shows these slopes vs offset frequency:

Region (offset)Slope
1 Hz – 100 Hz−30 dB/decade
100 Hz – 100 kHz−20 dB/decade
100 kHz – 10 MHz0 dB/decade (floor)

(a) Identify the dominant noise mechanism in each region.

(b) Estimate the flicker corner and the half-loop bandwidth $\omega_0/2Q$.

Answer

Given / required: three slopes are measured: −30 dB/dec (1 Hz–100 Hz), −20 dB/dec (100 Hz–100 kHz) and 0 dB/dec (above 100 kHz). Match them to the noise mechanisms of the Leeson model (5.1.9) and read off the two corner frequencies.

Formulas and sources(the Leeson model 5.1.9, full form):

$$\mathcal{L}(f_m) = \frac{FkT_0}{2P_0}\left(1+\frac{f_c}{f_m}\right)\left[1+\left(\frac{f_0}{2Q_L f_m}\right)^2\right]$$

Multiplying out the two brackets produces four slope regions: $f^{-3}$ (flicker × resonator), $f^{-2}$ (thermal × resonator), $f^{-1}$ (flicker passed straight through, often masked) and $f^0$ (the thermal floor).

(a) Matching the mechanisms:

  • −30 dB/dec($f^{-3}$): the device's $1/f$ flicker noise is upconverted close to the carrier and amplified by the tank's $1/f_m^2$ resonance — flicker-FM.
  • −20 dB/dec($f^{-2}$): thermal (white) noise falling inside the resonator half-bandwidth is amplified by the $\left(f_0/2Q_Lf_m\right)^2$ term — thermal-FM.
  • 0 dB/dec($f^0$): beyond the resonator half-bandwidth the noise is no longer amplified by the feedback, leaving the additive thermal floor $FkT_0/2P_0$.

(b) Reading off the corners:

The $f^{-3}\to f^{-2}$ boundary = the flicker corner:$f_c \approx 100\,\text{Hz}$(i.e. where $f_c/f_m = 1$).

The $f^{-2}\to f^{0}$ boundary = the resonator half-bandwidth:$\dfrac{f_0}{2Q_L} \approx 100\,\text{kHz}$(i.e. where $f_0/(2Q_Lf_m)=1$).

Working Q backwards (assuming $f_0 = 1\,\text{GHz}$): $Q_L = \dfrac{f_0}{2\times 100\,\text{kHz}} = \dfrac{10^9}{2\times 10^5} = 5000$.

flicker corner ≈ 100 Hz; f₀/2Q ≈ 100 kHz → if f₀ = 1 GHz then Q ≈ 5000 (implies a crystal or very high-Q cavity).

Physical interpretation: A phase-noise plot lets you diagnose an oscillator without opening it up: the flicker corner reflects the transistor process (about 1–10 kHz for a BJT, up to MHz for CMOS), and the half-bandwidth corner gives the loaded Q directly. A flicker corner as low as 100 Hz together with Q = 5000 is the signature of a crystal oscillator.

4.2 Medium
Quick Leeson estimate

An LC oscillator at $f_0 = 2.4\,\text{GHz}$ has loaded $Q = 12$, $F = 6\,\text{dB}$, output power $P_0 = 0\,\text{dBm}$.

(a) Estimate the phase-noise floor in the $1/f^2$ region at 600 kHz offset.

(b) What loaded $Q$ would be needed to drop the noise to −110 dBc/Hz at 600 kHz?

Answer

Given / required: $f_0 = 2.4\,\text{GHz}$, $Q = 12$, $F = 6\,\text{dB}$, $P_0 = 0\,\text{dBm}$ and an offset $f_m = 600\,\text{kHz}$. Find (a) $\mathcal{L}$ in the $1/f^2$ region; (b) the Q needed to reach −110 dBc/Hz.

Formulas and sources(the Leeson model 5.1.9, $1/f^2$ region):

$$\mathcal{L}(f_m) \approx \frac{kT_0 F}{2P_0}\left(\frac{f_0}{2Q f_m}\right)^2$$

(a) Substituting step by step(converting dB to linear first):

  • $F = 6\,\text{dB} \Rightarrow 10^{0.6} \approx 4$
  • $P_0 = 0\,\text{dBm} \Rightarrow 1\,\text{mW} = 10^{-3}\,\text{W}$
  • $kT_0 = 4\times 10^{-21}$ W·s

The noise-floor term: $\dfrac{kT_0F}{2P_0} = \dfrac{4\times 10^{-21}\times 4}{2\times 10^{-3}} = 8\times 10^{-18}\,/\text{Hz}$ (i.e. $10\log = -171\,\text{dBc/Hz}$).

The resonant amplification term: $\dfrac{f_0}{2Qf_m} = \dfrac{2.4\times 10^9}{2\times 12\times 6\times 10^5} = \dfrac{2.4\times 10^9}{1.44\times 10^7} = 167$; and $20\log(167) = +44.4\,\text{dB}$.

$\mathcal{L} = 8\times 10^{-18}\times 167^2 = 8\times 10^{-18}\times 2.78\times 10^4 = 2.22\times 10^{-13}\,/\text{Hz}$.

In dB: $10\log(2.22\times 10^{-13}) = 10\times(0.346 - 13) = -126.5$; checking in dB: $-171 + 44.4 = -126.6$ ✓.

𝓛(600 kHz) ≈ −126.5 dBc/Hz. [Erratum: the original answer gives −96.5 dBc/Hz, a 30 dB logarithm error from misreading 2.22×10⁻¹³ as 10⁻⁹·⁶⁵; the correct value is −126.5.]

(b) The target is $\mathcal{L}_\text{new} = -110\,\text{dBc/Hz} = 10^{-11}\,/\text{Hz}$. In the $1/f^2$ region $\mathcal{L} \propto 1/Q^2$, with the other parameters fixed:

$$Q_\text{new} = Q_\text{old}\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{L}_\text{old}}{\mathcal{L}_\text{new}}} = 12\sqrt{\frac{2.22\times 10^{-13}}{10^{-11}}} = 12\sqrt{0.0222} = 12\times 0.149 = \mathbf{1.8}$$

Because the corrected result of (a), −126.5 dBc/Hz, is already 16.5 dB better than −110, “reaching −110” requires only $Q \geq 1.8$ — and the existing $Q = 12$ carries $20\log(12/1.8) = 16.5\,\text{dB}$ of margin. [The original answer started from the erroneous −96.5 and concluded “Q ≈ 56 required”; with the correct numbers that conclusion does not hold.]

Q_min ≈ 1.8; Q = 12 already meets −110 dBc/Hz @ 600 kHz, with a margin of ≈ 16.5 dB.

Physical interpretation: A sanity check: a commercial 2.4 GHz LC-VCO measures about −120 to −125 dBc/Hz @ 1 MHz, which scaled to 600 kHz (+4.4 dB) is about −116 to −121 — a few dB worse than the pure-thermal Leeson prediction (−126.5), because under large-signal oscillation $F$ is an empirical parameter and is in practice higher than the small-signal NF. Leeson gives an optimistic lower bound; a design usually leaves a further 5–10 dB of margin.

4.3 Design
Design a low-phase-noise reference oscillator
Spec $f_0 = 10\,\text{GHz}$, $P_0 = +10\,\text{dBm}$
Phase noise: ≤ −110 dBc/Hz at 10 kHz offset (in the $1/f^2$ region)
Operating temperature: 0..+85 °C

(a) Compute the minimum unloaded Q required (assume $F = 10\,\text{dB}$ for the amplifier and that loaded Q ≈ unloaded Q/2 due to coupling).

(b) Suggest a resonator technology that achieves this Q (LC, ceramic, dielectric resonator, sapphire whispering-gallery, …).

Answer

Given / required: $f_0 = 10\,\text{GHz}$, $P_0 = +10\,\text{dBm}$, $F = 10\,\text{dB}$, a specification of $\mathcal{L}(10\,\text{kHz}) \leq -110\,\text{dBc/Hz}$ (in the $1/f^2$ region), and loaded $Q \approx$ unloaded $Q/2$. Find the minimum unloaded Q and a suitable resonator technology.

Formulas and sources(the Leeson model 5.1.9, solved for Q):

$$\mathcal{L}(f_m) = \frac{kT_0F}{2P_0}\left(\frac{f_0}{2Q_Lf_m}\right)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; Q_L \geq \frac{f_0}{2f_m}\sqrt{\frac{kT_0F}{2P_0\,\mathcal{L}}}$$

(a) Substituting step by step(dB → linear):

  • $\mathcal{L} = 10^{-110/10} = 10^{-11}\,/\text{Hz}$
  • $F = 10\,\text{dB} \Rightarrow 10$
  • $P_0 = +10\,\text{dBm} \Rightarrow 10\,\text{mW} = 10^{-2}\,\text{W}$

The noise-floor term: $\dfrac{kT_0F}{2P_0} = \dfrac{4\times 10^{-21}\times 10}{2\times 10^{-2}} = 2\times 10^{-18}\,/\text{Hz}$ (= −177 dBc/Hz).

The permissible resonant amplification: $\left(\dfrac{f_0}{2Q_Lf_m}\right)^2 \leq \dfrac{10^{-11}}{2\times 10^{-18}} = 5\times 10^6$, and taking the square root → $\dfrac{f_0}{2Q_Lf_m} \leq 2236$.

$$Q_L \geq \frac{f_0}{2\times 2236\times f_m} = \frac{10^{10}}{2\times 2236\times 10^4} = \frac{10^{10}}{4.47\times 10^7} = 224$$

Coupling correction (loaded ≈ unloaded/2, since half the energy is drawn off by the external load):

$$Q_\text{unloaded} \geq 2\times 224 = \mathbf{448}$$

(b) A survey of resonator technologies(10 GHz, Q ≥ 450):

  • Dielectric resonator (DR): typical Q = 5000–30,000 — comfortably meets the requirement and is the first choice.
  • Cavity resonator: Q = 1000–5000 — feasible but bulky.
  • SAW: limited to about 2 GHz, so unsuitable at 10 GHz.
  • LC on-chip: Q ≈ 10–20 — some 25× short, ruled out immediately.
  • Sapphire whispering-gallery: Q > 10⁵ — overkill, used for ultra-low-noise references such as atomic clocks.
Q_unloaded ≥ 448 → Recommended: DR-stabilized 10 GHz oscillator (DRO). Common in radar LO and SATCOM applications.

Physical interpretation: 10 kHz is a “close-in” offset, and the term $\left(f_0/2Qf_m\right)$ is enormous when $f_0/f_m = 10^6$, so the Q requirement is more than two orders of magnitude above that of P1.3 (a 100 kHz offset at a lower $f_0$). Note also that $P_0$ has already been pushed to +10 dBm to help hold the floor down: close-in specifications are almost always dominated by Q, since power buys dB only linearly whereas Q buys them quadratically.

Topic 5Receiver Phase Noise & Reciprocal Mixing

Translation of LO phase noise into IF noise; specifying LO from RX selectivity targets.

5.1 Easy
Direct formula application
$\mathcal{L}(\Delta f) \leq C - S - I - 10\log B$ (all dB).

A receiver needs $S = 15\,\text{dB}$ SIR for a desired signal $C = -90\,\text{dBm}$ with a blocker $I = -30\,\text{dBm}$ at $\Delta f = 200\,\text{kHz}$ offset. IF bandwidth $B = 30\,\text{kHz}$.

Find the required LO phase noise.

Answer

Given / required: a desired signal $C = -90\,\text{dBm}$, a blocker $I = -30\,\text{dBm}$ @ $\Delta f = 200\,\text{kHz}$, a required SIR of $S = 15\,\text{dB}$ and an IF bandwidth $B = 30\,\text{kHz}$. Find the upper limit on the LO phase noise at 200 kHz offset.

Formulas and sources(reciprocal mixing, 5.1.10): the LO's phase-noise skirt downconverts the blocker into noise falling within the IF bandwidth, of power $= I + \mathcal{L}(\Delta f) + 10\log B$ (adding in dB). That noise must be $S$ below the desired signal:

$$I + \mathcal{L}(\Delta f) + 10\log B \leq C - S \;\Rightarrow\; \mathcal{L}(\Delta f) \leq C - S - I - 10\log B$$

Substituting step by step:First the bandwidth term: $10\log(30{,}000) = 10\log(3\times 10^4) = 10(0.477+4) = 44.77\,\text{dB}$.

$$\mathcal{L} \leq -90 - 15 - (-30) - 44.77 = -90 - 15 + 30 - 44.77 = \mathbf{-119.8\,\text{dBc/Hz}}\ @\ 200\,\text{kHz}$$

𝓛(200 kHz) ≤ −119.8 dBc/Hz ≈ −120 dBc/Hz

Physical interpretation: The direction of every term can be sanity-checked: the stronger the blocker ($I\uparrow$), the higher the SIR required ($S\uparrow$), and the wider the bandwidth ($B\uparrow$, collecting more skirt noise) → the tighter the phase-noise limit; the stronger the desired signal ($C\uparrow$) → the more relaxed. −120 dBc/Hz @ 200 kHz is rather tight for a free-running LC-VCO and is usually achieved with a PLL plus a high-Q tank.

5.2 Medium
GSM phase-noise spec (Pozar's example, verify)

GSM requires 9 dB rejection of interferers at: $-23\,\text{dBm}@3\,\text{MHz}$, $-33\,\text{dBm}@1.6\,\text{MHz}$, $-43\,\text{dBm}@0.6\,\text{MHz}$, with carrier $C = -99\,\text{dBm}$ and $B = 200\,\text{kHz}$.

Compute the required phase noise at each offset and identify which is the most demanding spec.

Answer

Given / required: the GSM blocking template: carrier $C = -99\,\text{dBm}$, $B = 200\,\text{kHz}$, blocker powers at each offset as given, and a rejection of $S = 9\,\text{dB}$. Find the $\mathcal{L}$ specification at each offset and identify the most demanding one.

Formulas and sources(reciprocal mixing,5.1.10):

$$\mathcal{L}(\Delta f) \leq C - S - I - 10\log B$$

Substituting step by step:The bandwidth term $10\log(200{,}000) = 10\log(2\times 10^5) = 10(0.301+5) = 53.0\,\text{dB}$. The three offsets share $C$, $S$ and $B$, so combine the constants first:

$$\mathcal{L} = -99 - 9 - I - 53 = -161 - I \quad(I\ \text{substituted in dBm})$$

Row by row: $I = -23 \Rightarrow \mathcal{L} = -161+23 = -138$; $I = -33 \Rightarrow -128$; $I = -43 \Rightarrow -118$:

5.3 Design

(b) Is this achievable with a typical 2.4 GHz LC-VCO ($Q\approx 10$)? Use Leeson with $F=5\,\text{dB}$, $P_0 = -5\,\text{dBm}$.

Answer

Given / required: $C = -70\,\text{dBm}$, $S = 21\,\text{dB}$, a WiFi blocker $I = +10\,\text{dBm}$ @ $\Delta f = 22\,\text{MHz}$, $B = 1\,\text{MHz}$; the candidate VCO has $f_0 = 2.4\,\text{GHz}$, $Q = 10$, $F = 5\,\text{dB}$, $P_0 = -5\,\text{dBm}$. Find (a) the phase-noise requirement and (b) whether it can be met.

(a) Reciprocal mixing(5.1.10): the bandwidth term is $10\log(10^6) = 60\,\text{dB}$.

$$\mathcal{L} \leq C - S - I - 10\log B = -70 - 21 - (+10) - 60 = \mathbf{-161\,\text{dBc/Hz}}\ @\ 22\,\text{MHz}$$

Note that $I$ is apositive +10 dBm (the WiFi at the next table); that term alone makes this 40 dB harder than the scenario of P5.1.

(b) Checking against Leeson(5.1.9, $1/f^2$ region). Convert to linear first:

  • $F = 5\,\text{dB} \Rightarrow 10^{0.5} = 3.16$
  • $P_0 = -5\,\text{dBm} \Rightarrow 10^{-0.5}\,\text{mW} = 0.316\,\text{mW} = 3.16\times 10^{-4}\,\text{W}$

The noise-floor term: $\dfrac{kT_0F}{2P_0} = \dfrac{4\times 10^{-21}\times 3.16}{2\times 3.16\times 10^{-4}} = 2\times 10^{-17}\,/\text{Hz}$ (= −167 dBc/Hz).

6.1 Easy
Integer-N output frequency

An integer-N PLL uses $f_\text{ref} = 200\,\text{kHz}$ and is tuned across the GSM 900 MHz uplink band (880–915 MHz).

(a) What range of divider values $N$ is needed?

(b) How many discrete channels can it generate?

Answer

Given / required: an integer-N PLL with $f_\text{ref} = 200\,\text{kHz}$ covering an output of 880–915 MHz. Find the divider range and the number of channels.

Formulas and sources(the integer-N PLL, 5.2.2): when locked, the two phase-detector inputs are at the same frequency, $f_\text{out}/N = f_\text{ref}$, so

$$f_\text{out} = N\,f_\text{ref},\qquad N = \frac{f_\text{out}}{f_\text{ref}}$$

Substituting step by step:

(a) At the lower edge: $N = \dfrac{880\times 10^6}{2\times 10^5} = 4400$; at the upper edge: $N = \dfrac{915\times 10^6}{2\times 10^5} = 4575$. Both divide exactly ✓ (GSM channels lie on a 200 kHz raster). Range: N = 4400..4575of the image-reject mixer at low IF.

Physical interpretation: Hidden here is integer-N's fundamental trade-off: a fine channel raster (200 kHz) forces a low $f_\text{ref}$, whereupon (i) the loop bandwidth is limited to about $f_\text{ref}/10 = 20\,\text{kHz}$ → slow switching; and (ii) $N$ reaches ~4500 → in-band noise amplified by $20\log N = 73\,\text{dB}$ (see P6.2). These two pain points are exactly why fractional-N (5.2.3) exists.

6.2 Medium
Phase-noise penalty of division ratio N

The reference oscillator has $\mathcal{L}_\text{ref}(1\,\text{kHz}) = -160\,\text{dBc/Hz}$.

(a) What is the in-band PLL output phase noise at $\Delta f = 1\,\text{kHz}$ for $N = 4500$ (GSM example)?

(b) Compare with a fractional-N PLL where $N_\text{frac} = 35$ (using $f_\text{ref} = 26\,\text{MHz}$).

Answer

Given / required: the reference has $\mathcal{L}_\text{ref}(1\,\text{kHz}) = -160\,\text{dBc/Hz}$. Find the in-band (within the loop bandwidth) output phase noise for (a) integer-N with $N=4500$ and (b) fractional-N with $N_\text{frac}=35$.

Formulas and sources(PLL in-band noise, 5.2.2): within the loop bandwidth the PLL locks the output into an $N$-times copy of the reference phase — the phase is multiplied by $N$, so the noisepoweris multiplied by $N^2$:

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{out}(f_m) = \mathcal{L}_\text{ref}(f_m) + 20\log N \quad (f_m < f_\text{BW})$$

The intuitive reason: $\phi_\text{out} = N\,\phi_\text{ref}$ (multiply the frequency by $N$ and the phase is multiplied by $N$ too), and power $\propto \phi^2$.

Substituting step by step:

(a) $20\log(4500) = 20\times 3.653 = +73.1\,\text{dB}$.

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{out} = -160 + 73 = \mathbf{-87\,\text{dBc/Hz}}\ @\ 1\,\text{kHz}$$

(b) Fractional-N uses $f_\text{ref} = 26\,\text{MHz}$ directly as the phase-detector frequency with $N_\text{frac} = 35$ (the output is still about $35\times 26 = 910\,\text{MHz}$ — the same output frequency!):

$20\log(35) = 20\times 1.544 = +30.9\,\text{dB}$.

$$\mathcal{L}_\text{out} = -160 + 31 = \mathbf{-129\,\text{dBc/Hz}}\ @\ 1\,\text{kHz}$$

The gap: $-87 - (-129) = 42\,\text{dB} = 20\log(4500/35)$ — entirely from the ratio of the divisors.

Integer-N: −87 dBc/Hz; fractional-N: −129 dBc/Hz. Fractional-N improves close-in phase noise by 42 dB by avoiding the huge integer division ratio — the key reason modern wireless transceivers use fractional-N.

Physical interpretation: The same reference and the same output frequency, yet simply because the phase detector runs at 26 MHz instead of 200 kHz, the close-in noise differs by 42 dB. The price is the quantization (ΔΣ) spurs of the fractional divisor, which need dithering and loop filtering (5.2.3, P8.4) — but that is a far easier price to pay than 42 dB.

6.3 Easy
Fractional-N output and resolution

A fractional-N synthesizer has $f_\text{ref} = 19.2\,\text{MHz}$, integer part $N = 156$, fractional accumulator size $R = 2^{24}$, current fractional value $K = 5\,592\,405$.

(a) Compute $f_\text{out}$.

(b) What is the frequency resolution?

Answer

Given / required: $f_\text{ref} = 19.2\,\text{MHz}$, $N = 156$, an accumulator of $R = 2^{24} = 16{,}777{,}216$ and $K = 5{,}592{,}405$. Find $f_\text{out}$ and the frequency resolution.

Formulas and sources(fractional-N, 5.2.3): the accumulator switches the divisor between $N$ and $N+1$ with a duty cycle of $K/R$, giving anaveragedivisor of $N + K/R$:

$$f_\text{out} = \left(N + \frac{K}{R}\right) f_\text{ref},\qquad \Delta f_\text{min} = \frac{f_\text{ref}}{R}$$

Substituting step by step:

(a) The fractional part: $\dfrac{K}{R} = \dfrac{5{,}592{,}405}{16{,}777{,}216} = 0.333333$ (deliberately chosen as $\approx 2^{24}/3$, i.e. 1/3).

$$f_\text{out} = (156 + 0.3333)\times 19.2\,\text{MHz} = 156.3333\times 19.2 = \mathbf{3001.6\,MHz} \approx 3\,\text{GHz}$$

(Check: $156\times 19.2 = 2995.2$ and $0.3333\times 19.2 = 6.4$, totalling 3001.6 ✓)

(b) $$\Delta f = \frac{f_\text{ref}}{R} = \frac{19.2\times 10^6}{1.678\times 10^7} = \mathbf{1.14\,Hz}$$

f_out = 3001.6 MHz; resolution = 1.14 Hz — the phase detector still runs at 19.2 MHz, yet the resolution is at the Hz level.

Physical interpretation: This is precisely the magic of fractional-N: integer-N would need $f_\text{ref} = 1\,\text{Hz}$ for 1 Hz resolution (utterly impractical), whereas fractional-N gets it “for free” with a 24-bit accumulator. The price: the instantaneous phase error created by switching between $N$ and $N{+}1$ is periodic → fractional spurs (especially when $K/R$ is near a simple fraction such as 1/3), requiring ΔΣ noise shaping to push them to high frequencies where the loop filter absorbs them.

6.4 Design
Spec a fractional-N PLL for LTE Band 1

Settling time (frequency hop): ≤ 200 μs
Reference: $f_\text{ref} = 38.4\,\text{MHz}$ (TCXO)

(a) Compute integer-N range and the fractional accumulator size to achieve 1 kHz resolution.

(b) Verify settling time. Rule of thumb: settling ≈ 5/(loop BW).

(c) Discuss whether integer-N (no fractional) could meet the same resolution with $f_\text{ref} = 38.4\,\text{MHz}$.

Answer

Given / required: an output of 2110–2170 MHz, 1 kHz resolution, settling ≤ 200 µs, $f_\text{ref} = 38.4\,\text{MHz}$ and a loop bandwidth of 100 kHz. Find (a) the N range and accumulator size, (b) verify the settling, and (c) assess the feasibility of integer-N.

(a) N range and resolution(fractional-N,5.2.3):$N = f_\text{out}/f_\text{ref}$:

Lower edge: $N = \dfrac{2110}{38.4} = 54.95$; upper edge: $N = \dfrac{2170}{38.4} = 56.51$ → an integer part of 54..57, with the fractional part supplied by the accumulator.

The resolution condition: $\Delta f = \dfrac{f_\text{ref}}{R} \leq 1\,\text{kHz} \Rightarrow R \geq \dfrac{38.4\times 10^6}{10^3} = 38{,}400$.

Take a power of 2: $R = 2^{16} = 65{,}536 \Rightarrow \Delta f = \dfrac{38.4\times 10^6}{65{,}536} = \mathbf{586\,Hz}$ (better than 1 kHz; over-resolving does no harm).

(b) Settling time:Rule of thumb: settling ≈ $5/\omega_\text{BW}$ (the angular-frequency version) or $5/f_\text{BW}$ (the conservative version):

$$t_s \approx \frac{5}{2\pi\times 10^5} = 7.96\,\mu\text{s}\quad\text{(even conservatively, } 5/f_\text{BW} = 50\,\mu\text{s}\text{)} \ll 200\,\mu\text{s} \checkmark$$

Checking the loop bandwidth is legitimate: $f_\text{PD} = 38.4\,\text{MHz}$ and $f_\text{BW} = 100\,\text{kHz} = f_\text{PD}/384 \ll f_\text{PD}/10$ ✓ (ample stability margin).

(c) The integer-N counterexample:For 1 kHz resolution, integer-N is forced to $f_\text{ref} = f_\text{PD} = 1\,\text{kHz}$, whereupon

$$N = \frac{2.17\times 10^9}{10^3} = 2.17\times 10^6 \;\Rightarrow\; 20\log N = +126.7\,\text{dB}$$

Even with a reference at $-160\,\text{dBc/Hz}$, the in-band output is $= -160 + 127 = \mathbf{-33\,\text{dBc/Hz}}$ — completely unusable (even the SSB signal itself would be swamped by phase noise). And the loop bandwidth ≤ 100 Hz → settling of tens of ms, violating the 200 µs. Fractional-N isnecessaryhere, not optional.

N = 54–57 (integer) + an R = 2¹⁶ accumulator (586 Hz); settling ~8 µs ≪ 200 µs. Modern cellular synthesizers use Σ-Δ fractional-N to combine 1 kHz resolution with reference frequencies of tens of MHz.

Physical interpretation: The 127 dB figure in (c) is worth remembering: integer-N's noise penalty $20\log N$ scales directly with the ratio “output frequency / resolution”, and at cellular grade (a GHz output with kHz resolution) that ratio of ~10⁶ rules integer-N out completely. Fractional-N decouples “resolution” from “phase-detector frequency”, which is the core message of 5.2.3.

Topic 7Direct Digital Synthesis (DDS)

Phase accumulator + sine LUT + DAC + LPF; FTW and resolution.

7.1 Easy
FTW and DDS output frequency

A 32-bit DDS is clocked at $f_\text{clk} = 500\,\text{MHz}$.

(a) Find the FTW for exactly $f_\text{out} = 75\,\text{MHz}$.

(b) What is the minimum frequency step (1-LSB resolution)?

Answer

Given / required: a 32-bit DDS with $f_\text{clk} = 500\,\text{MHz}$ and a target $f_\text{out} = 75\,\text{MHz}$. Find the FTW and the 1-LSB resolution.

Formulas and sources(the DDS phase accumulator, 5.2.4): on every clock cycle the phase accumulator adds the FTW, and one full wrap of $2^{32}$ is one output period:

$$f_\text{out} = \frac{\text{FTW}}{2^{32}}\,f_\text{clk} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{FTW} = \frac{f_\text{out}}{f_\text{clk}}\cdot 2^{32},\qquad \Delta f_\text{LSB} = \frac{f_\text{clk}}{2^{32}}$$

Substituting step by step:

(a) The frequency ratio: $\dfrac{f_\text{out}}{f_\text{clk}} = \dfrac{75}{500} = 0.15$. And $2^{32} = 4{,}294{,}967{,}296$.

$$\text{FTW} = 0.15\times 4{,}294{,}967{,}296 = 644{,}245{,}094.4 \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf{644{,}245{,}094}\ (\text{rounded})$$

Since 0.15 is not a combination of $2^{-k}$, the FTW must be rounded → the actual output is $= \dfrac{644{,}245{,}094}{2^{32}}\times 500\,\text{MHz} = 74.999{,}999{,}95\,\text{MHz}$, an error of only $0.4\,\text{LSB} \approx 0.047\,\text{Hz}$ (0.6 ppb).

(b) $$\Delta f_\text{LSB} = \frac{5\times 10^8}{4.295\times 10^9} = \mathbf{0.1164\,Hz}$$

FTW = 644,245,094 (= 0x2666_6666); resolution = 0.116 Hz — sub-Hz resolution is the hallmark of DDS.

Physical interpretation: A DDS's frequency accuracy is limited only by FTW quantization (< 0.06 Hz) and the accuracy of the clock itself, and changing frequency means “writing one register”, takes effect within a single clock cycle and isphase-continuous — three things a PLL cannot do. The costs are in P7.2/P7.3: the output frequency ceiling and DAC spurs.

7.2 Medium
DDS maximum output frequency

The same 500 MHz DDS is used in a swept-source instrument.

(a) What is the theoretical maximum output frequency? (Nyquist limit.)

(b) Practical maximum is usually ~40% of $f_\text{clk}$ due to image-rejection filter roll-off. Compute the practical maximum and explain why.

(c) For wider sweep, would you increase $f_\text{clk}$ or use multiple DDS in parallel?

Answer

Given / required: a DDS with $f_\text{clk} = 500\,\text{MHz}$. Find the theoretical and practical maximum output frequencies, and compare the two routes to more bandwidth.

(a) The Nyquist ceiling(the sampling theorem): a DDS output is a sampled signal, and the highest reconstructable frequency is

$$f_\text{max,theory} = \frac{f_\text{clk}}{2} = \frac{500}{2} = \mathbf{250\,MHz}$$

(b) The practical ceiling (~40%):$0.4\times 500 = \mathbf{200\,MHz}$. The reason is that the DAC's first image sits at $f_\text{clk} - f_\text{out}$:

  • at $f_\text{out} = 200\,\text{MHz}$ the image is at $500-200 = 300\,\text{MHz}$ → the reconstruction LPF has a transition band of 200→300 MHz (a 1.5:1 ratio), which is realizable.
  • at $f_\text{out} = 240\,\text{MHz}$ the image is at $260\,\text{MHz}$ → the transition band is only 240→260 MHz (8% relative width), demanding an extremely high-order filter, with worsening passband ripple and delay distortion.

In addition, the DAC's $\text{sinc}$ roll-off ($\sin(\pi f/f_\text{clk})/(\pi f/f_\text{clk})$) costs 3.92 dB at Nyquist and about 2.4 dB at 200 MHz — the closer to Nyquist, the weaker the signal and the stronger the image, compounding the problem.

(c) Strategies for more bandwidth:Raising $f_\text{clk}$ is the clean route — the bandwidth and the image spacing scale together, leaving the filter's proportions unchanged. Modern DDS ICs (the AD9914/9915 and similar) run at 3.5–4 GS/s. Paralleling several DDSs (polyphase/interleaving) requires solving phase synchronization and gain/timing calibration, and is used only in ultra-wideband instruments.

Theoretically 250 MHz (Nyquist); practically ≈ 200 MHz (40% of f_clk, limited by the image-filter transition band).

Physical interpretation: The “40% rule” is essentiallyfilter physicsrather than mathematics: Nyquist says 250 MHz is allowed, but you would need a brick-wall filter to collect it. This also explains why a DDS is usually used as a fine reference or IF source for a PLL, leaving the GHz carrier to the PLL (see P8.4c).

Hop time: < 100 ns (between clock cycles)
Spurious-free dynamic range (SFDR): ≥ 75 dBc within 10% of carrier

(a) Choose the DDS clock $f_\text{clk}$.

(b) Determine the minimum accumulator width $M$.

(c) The DAC SFDR sets the spur floor. What DAC resolution (N-bit) is needed?

Answer

Given / required: an output of 100 MHz–1.2 GHz, resolution ≤ 10 Hz, hop < 100 ns and SFDR ≥ 75 dBc. Find $f_\text{clk}$, the accumulator width $M$ and the DAC resolution $N$.

(a) Choosing the clock(the 40% rule of P7.2): the highest output is 1.2 GHz, so

$$f_\text{clk} \geq \frac{f_\text{out,max}}{0.4} = \frac{1.2\,\text{GHz}}{0.4} = 3\,\text{GHz} \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{choose } f_\text{clk} = 3\,\text{GHz}$$

(The lowest output of 100 MHz is then only $f_\text{clk}/30$, so the sinc roll-off and the images are both trivially easy.)

(b) Accumulator width(5.2.4):$\Delta f = \dfrac{f_\text{clk}}{2^M} \leq 10\,\text{Hz}$:

$$2^M \geq \frac{3\times 10^9}{10} = 3\times 10^8,\qquad \log_2(3\times 10^8) = \frac{\log_{10}(3\times 10^8)}{\log_{10}2} = \frac{8.477}{0.301} = 28.2$$

Take the next standard width, $\mathbf{M = 32\,bits}$ → $\Delta f = \dfrac{3\times 10^9}{2^{32}} = 0.70\,\text{Hz}$ (a 14× margin).

(c) DAC resolution:The rule of thumb for the quantization-harmonic ceiling is SFDR ≈ 6 dB/bit (strictly, $6.02N+1.76$ is the SNR; SFDR is a little better when the spurs are concentrated, but ~6 dB/bit remains the working estimate):

$$N \geq \frac{75}{6} = 12.5 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{14-bit DAC}$$

Theoretically ~84 dB; after deducting the 5–10 dB lost to package coupling, layout and clock feedthrough, the measured value is ~75 dB — only just meeting the specification, which is why 13 bits is not chosen.

Verifying the hop time:Changing frequency = writing a new FTW, effective in one clock cycle: $t = \dfrac{1}{3\times 10^9} = 0.33\,\text{ns} \ll 100\,\text{ns}$ ✓, and phase-continuous (preserving the coherence of a radar pulse train).

f_clk = 3 GHz, M = 32 bits (0.70 Hz), a 14-bit DAC. Realistic chip: Analog Devices AD9914 (3.5 GS/s, 12-bit, 32-bit accum) — matches all specs except SFDR (~70 dB typical), close but may need additional filtering.

Physical interpretation: This specification sheet is exactly the DDS sweet spot: a PLL must re-lock to change frequency (µs–ms), whereas a DDS does it in nanoseconds and phase-continuously — frequency-agile radar and EW systems simply have to use DDS. The bottleneck is always the DAC: demanding both speed (3 GS/s) and linearity (14 bits) drives up power and price.

(a) Size $C_1$ and $C_2$.

(b) Write the Barkhausen start-up condition for the Colpitts ($g_m/G_i \geq C_2/C_1$) and find the required $g_m$ with the ×3 margin.

(c) Estimate $\mathcal{L}(100\,\text{kHz})$ from Leeson's model.

(d) The spec is −125 dBc/Hz at 100 kHz. What $Q_L$ (alone) or what power increase (alone) closes the gap?

Answer

Given / required: a CE Colpitts with $L_3 = 12\,\text{nH}$, $f_0 = 915\,\text{MHz}$, $C_1 = C_2$, $G_i = 0.5\,\text{mS}$, a ×3 margin, $Q_L = 15$, $P = 0\,\text{dBm}$ and $F = 6\,\text{dB}$. Design chain: tank → start-up $g_m$ → Leeson → closing the specification gap.

(a) Designing the capacitors(the resonance condition, 5.1.4): solve $\omega_0^2 = 1/(L_3 C_\text{eq})$ backwards:

$\omega_0 = 2\pi\times 915\times 10^6 = 5.75\times 10^9$ rad/s, so $\omega_0^2 = 3.31\times 10^{19}$.

$$C_\text{eq} = \frac{1}{\omega_0^2 L_3} = \frac{1}{3.31\times 10^{19}\times 12\times 10^{-9}} = \frac{1}{3.97\times 10^{11}} = 2.52\,\text{pF}$$

$C_1 = C_2$ in series → $C_\text{eq} = C_1/2$ → $C_1 = C_2 = 2\times 2.52 = \mathbf{5.04\,pF}$ (take 5.1 pF, E24).

(b) Start-up $g_m$(Barkhausen 5.1.2 + the Colpitts gain condition): $g_{m,\min} = G_i\,\dfrac{C_2}{C_1} = 0.5\,\text{mS}\times 1 = 0.5\,\text{mS}$. With a ×3 margin:

g_m ≥ 3 × 0.5 = 1.5 mS — excess gain at small amplitude; the amplitude grows until the large-signal g_m(A) falls back to the Barkhausen value (limit cycle, 5.1.2)

(For a BJT: $g_m = I_C/V_T$ → $I_C \geq 1.5\,\text{mS}\times 26\,\text{mV} \approx 39\,\mu\text{A}$, extremely frugal.)

(c) The Leeson estimate(5.1.9, $1/f^2$ region), split into two terms in dB:

$$\mathcal{L}(f_m) = 10\log\!\left[\frac{FkT}{2P}\left(1 + \Big(\frac{f_0}{2Q_L f_m}\Big)^2\right)\right]$$

The resonant amplification term: $\dfrac{f_0}{2 Q_L f_m} = \dfrac{915\times 10^6}{2\times 15\times 10^5} = \dfrac{915\times 10^6}{3\times 10^6} = 305$ → $10\log(1+305^2) = 10\log(9.3\times 10^4) = 49.7\,\text{dB}$.

The noise-floor term: $kT = -174\,\text{dBm/Hz}$, $F = +6\,\text{dB}$, $P = 0\,\text{dBm}$, and $2P \Rightarrow +3\,\text{dB}$:

$$10\log\frac{FkT}{2P} = -174 + 6 - 3 = -171\,\text{dB}$$

L(100 kHz) = −171 + 49.7 = −121.3 dBc/Hz

(d) Closing the 3.7 dB gap(the specification being −125):

  • Adjusting Q alone: $\mathcal{L}\propto 1/Q_L^2$ (6 dB gained per doubling of Q) → $Q_L \geq 15\times 10^{3.7/20} = 15\times 1.53 = \mathbf{23}$.
  • Adjusting power alone: $\mathcal{L}\propto 1/P$ (dB for dB) → $P \geq 0 + 3.7 = +3.7\,\text{dBm} \approx 2.3\,\text{mW}$.

Physical interpretation: The exponents make it plain — Q pays back quadratically (every dB of effort buys 2 dB of noise) while power is merely linear (1:1) and burns current directly; so the design order is always to squeeze Q first (layout, inductor selection) and only add power if that is not enough. Taking Q from 15 → 23 here is feasible with an off-chip inductor but close to the limit on-chip.

8.2 Hard
One-port negative-resistance oscillator — load design, amplitude, Kurokawa
5.1.5 start-up rule → steady-state amplitude → 5.1.6 Kurokawa stability.
Device data (6 GHz) Small-signal input impedance toward the resonator: $Z_{in} = -44 - j25\ \Omega$
Amplitude dependence: $R_{in}(A) = -44\,(1 - A/2)\ \Omega$ ($A$ = RF voltage amplitude in volts); $X_{in}$ ≈ constant

(a) Choose the series load $Z_L = R_L + jX_L$ using the $R_L = |R_{in}|/3$ rule, and give the inductor value that realizes $X_L$.

(b) Why $|R_{in}|/3$? What do larger or smaller choices trade off?

(c) Find the steady-state oscillation amplitude $A_0$.

(d) Verify Kurokawa's stability condition at $A_0$.

Answer

Given / required: a 6 GHz one-port device with a small-signal $Z_{in} = -44 - j25\,\Omega$, an amplitude dependence $R_{in}(A) = -44(1-A/2)\,\Omega$ and $X_{in}$ treated as constant. Design chain: load design → the physics of the start-up criterion → steady-state amplitude → Kurokawa stability.

(a) Load design(start-up rule,5.1.5):

$$R_L = \frac{|R_{in}|}{3} = \frac{44}{3} = \mathbf{14.7\,\Omega}$$

The resonance (frequency) condition $X_L = -X_{in} = +25\,\Omega$ (inductive, cancelling the device's $-j25$):

$$L = \frac{X_L}{2\pi f} = \frac{25}{2\pi\times 6\times 10^9} = \frac{25}{3.77\times 10^{10}} = 6.6\times 10^{-10}\,\text{H} = \mathbf{0.66\,nH}\ \text{(series inductor)}$$

(At 6 GHz, 0.66 nH is already bond-wire / short-transmission-line territory — in practice a transmission-line stub is used directly.)

(b) Why $|R_{in}|/3$:Start-up needs a negative net resistance at small amplitude, $R_{in}(0) + R_L = -44 + 14.7 = -29.3\,\Omega < 0$; the more negative, the faster and more reliable the start-up. Taking 1/3 leaves 2/3 of the negative resistance as margin, covering temperature, bias and component spread. The trade-off:

  • $R_L \to |R_{in}|$: the margin disappears and it may fail to start at a process corner (as soon as the large-signal $|R_{in}(A)|$ shrinks below $R_L$).
  • $R_L \ll |R_{in}|/3$: available negative resistance is wasted — the steady-state power delivered to the load $\propto R_L A_0^2/2$ falls, and the amplitude must swing further before limiting, increasing harmonic distortion.

(c) Steady-state amplitude:The steady-state condition $R_{in}(A_0) + R_L = 0$, i.e. $|R_{in}(A_0)| = R_L$:

$$44\left(1-\frac{A_0}{2}\right) = 14.7 \;\Rightarrow\; 1-\frac{A_0}{2} = 0.334 \;\Rightarrow\; A_0 = 2(1-0.334) = \mathbf{1.33\,V}$$

(d) Kurokawa stability(5.1.6), with $T$ = the sum of device and load:

$$\frac{\partial R_T}{\partial A}\frac{\partial X_T}{\partial \omega} - \frac{\partial X_T}{\partial A}\frac{\partial R_T}{\partial \omega} > 0$$

Checking term by term:

  • $\dfrac{\partial R_T}{\partial A} = \dfrac{\partial R_{in}}{\partial A} = 44\times\dfrac{1}{2} = +22\,\Omega/\text{V} > 0$ (as the amplitude rises the negative resistance weakens).
  • $\dfrac{\partial X_T}{\partial A} \approx 0$ (the problem states $X_{in}$ does not vary with amplitude).
  • $\dfrac{\partial X_T}{\partial \omega} > 0$: the reactance slope of a series resonator (the L with the device capacitance) at resonance is necessarily positive ($\partial X/\partial\omega = L + 1/(\omega^2 C) > 0$).

So the first term is $(+)(+) > 0$ and the second is 0 → the condition holds ✓.

R_L = 14.7 Ω, L = 0.66 nH, A₀ = 1.33 V, Kurokawa ✓. Amplitude perturbations decay: a dip in A makes R_T more negative (regrowth), a bulge makes it positive (decay) — a stable limit cycle, the nonlinear stability of 5.1.2 restated in impedance form.

Physical interpretation: The Kurokawa condition simply says that the “amplitude restoring force × frequency selecting force” must share a sign: $\partial R_T/\partial A > 0$ provides the amplitude spring and $\partial X_T/\partial\omega > 0$ (a steep slope at high Q) the frequency spring. The higher the Q and the steeper $\partial X_T/\partial\omega$, the more firmly the operating point is held — two faces of the same thing as Leeson's low phase noise.

Physical interpretation: This problem is a complete LO design chain: system specification (the blocker) → device check (the VCO has 22 dB of margin, a pass) → architecture (integer-N cannot even reach −118: a dismal −76.6; fractional-N passes) → parameters (put the loop bandwidth at the crossover). When a synthesizer design question comes up in an exam, follow these four steps.

2.4 GHz BLE channel synthesizer — integer-N vs fractional-N vs DDS hybrid
5.2.1–5.2.4 against lock time and noise. Rules: loop BW ≤ $f_{PD}/10$, $t_\text{lock} \approx 4/f_\text{BW}$.
Spec Output 2.402–2.480 GHz in 1 MHz channels; crystal $f_\text{ref} = 16\,\text{MHz}$; channel-switch (lock) time < 40 µs
Fractional modulator: 20-bit; DDS option: usable output ≤ 0.4 $f_\text{clk}$, SFDR 70 dBc

(a) Integer-N: give $f_{PD}$, the N range, the maximum loop bandwidth, the lock time, and the in-band $20\log N$ penalty. Does it meet 40 µs?

(b) Fractional-N: give $f_{PD}$, the N range, the frequency resolution, the in-band improvement over (a), and the lock time for a 200 kHz loop.

(c) Why can't a DDS generate the carrier directly? If a 152.5 MHz DDS output is multiplied up to 2.44 GHz instead, what happens to its −70 dBc spurs?

(d) Recommend an architecture.

Answer

Given / required: an output of 2.402–2.480 GHz, 1 MHz channels, $f_\text{ref} = 16\,\text{MHz}$ and lock < 40 µs; the rules: loop BW ≤ $f_{PD}/10$ and $t_\text{lock} \approx 4/f_\text{BW}$; a 20-bit fractional part; a DDS ceiling of $0.4f_\text{clk}$ and SFDR of 70 dBc. Compare the three architectures.

(a) Integer-N:The channel spacing chains $f_{PD} = 1\,\text{MHz}$:

$N = \dfrac{f_\text{out}}{f_{PD}} = \dfrac{2402\text{–}2480\,\text{MHz}}{1\,\text{MHz}} = \mathbf{2402\text{–}2480}$.

Loop BW $\leq \dfrac{f_{PD}}{10} = 100\,\text{kHz}$ → $t_\text{lock} \approx \dfrac{4}{10^5} = \mathbf{40\,\mu s}$ — Exactly on the line, with zero margin(any drift in the loop parameters and it overruns). In-band penalty: $20\log(2440) = 67.7\,\text{dB}$.

(b) Fractional-N:$f_{PD} = f_\text{ref} = 16\,\text{MHz}$:

$N = \dfrac{2402}{16} = 150.125$ to $\dfrac{2480}{16} = 155.000$ → N = 150.125–155.000(the fractional part realized by the ΔΣ modulator).

Resolution: $\dfrac{16\times 10^6}{2^{20}} = \dfrac{16\times 10^6}{1.049\times 10^6} = \mathbf{15.3\,Hz} \ll 1\,\text{MHz}$ ✓ (the channel raster comes free, and the Hz-level resolution incidentally also compensates the crystal's ppm error).

In-band: $20\log(152.5) = 43.7\,\text{dB}$ → against integer-N's 67.7 dB, that is 24 dB quieterof the image-reject mixer at low IF.

Lock: the $f_{PD}/10$ ceiling rises to 1.6 MHz, but ΔΣ quantization noise rises at high offsets, so the practical loop ceiling is ~200 kHz → $t_\text{lock} \approx \dfrac{4}{2\times 10^5} = \mathbf{20\,\mu s}$ — a 2× margin ✓.

(c) The DDS route:Direct generation would need $f_\text{clk} \geq \dfrac{2.48\,\text{GHz}}{0.4} = 6.2\,\text{GHz}$ — the power and cost of a 6 GS/s DAC are wholly disproportionate for a BLE SoC (5.2.4). Take the “low-frequency DDS × multiplication” route instead: multiplying 152.5 MHz by 16 up to 2.44 GHz multiplies the phase (and hence the spurs) by 16 as well:

$$\text{spur degradation} = 20\log 16 = 24.1\,\text{dB} \;\Rightarrow\; -70 + 24.1 = \mathbf{-45.9\,dBc}$$

Spurs at −46 dBc will not pass a typical adjacent-channel specification — a DDS-as-reference scheme must either keep the multiplication factor low or narrowband-filter the DDS output.

(d) Architecture recommendation:

Fractional-N PLL: meets resolution (15 Hz), lock (20 µs with margin), and in-band noise (24 dB better than integer-N); manage ΔΣ spurs via loop bandwidth and dithering. This is exactly what production BLE SoCs use.

Physical interpretation: Each of the three routes founders on its own physics: integer-N on the iron chain “channel spacing = $f_{PD}$” (zero lock-time margin plus 68 dB of noise amplification); DDS on DAC speed and the $20\log N$ degradation of spurs under multiplication; fractional-N converts both problems into “high-frequency quantization noise the loop filter can absorb” using a digital ΔΣ — and digital circuitry is the cheapest, so it wins.
