What every element in the figure means

① The two input ports: RF input and LO input

The two arrows on the left inject, respectively:

  • RF input(blue, entering port 1): the radio-frequency signal to be downconverted, $v_{RF}(t)=V_{RF}\cos\omega_{RF}t$.
  • LO input(red, entering port 4): the local-oscillator signal $v_{LO}(t)=V_{LO}\cos\omega_{LO}t$, which is responsible for switching the diodes.
Why don't the RF and LO interfere with each other?
Because the 3 dB 90° hybrid has $S_{14}=S_{41}=0$ — the RF and LO ports are inherently isolated, so no external isolator is needed.
Terminology: what exactly is a “hybrid”?

Hybrid(hybrid junction / hybrid coupler) is the microwave-engineering name for a particular class of four-port passive component for a component whose function is to take one signal and into two, or combines two signals into one, while strictly controlling the phase difference and power ratio.

The four defining properties

  1. Four ports (4-port), each of which, taken on its own, is perfectly matched($S_{ii}=0$).
  2. Equal power split(3 dB): a signal incident on any port → each of the two output ports takes half the power (the voltage amplitude becomes $1/\sqrt{2}$).
  3. There is a fixed phase difference($90°$ or $180°$, depending on the implementation).
  4. The remaining port is completely isolated(the signal does not pass through to it).

Where the name comes from

The 19th-century telephone hybrid was a 2-wire-to-4-wire coupler that let a single telephone line carry both the receive and transmit directions at once — it “hybridized” two electrical paths into one, hence the name. RF engineers kept the name for any component exhibiting similar “four-port controlled mixed splitting” behaviour.

Its role in slide 26

The “3 dB 90° hybrid” is a quadrature coupler — with the RF incident at port 1, ports 2 and 3 each take half the power (with a $90°$ phase difference) and port 4 (LO) is isolated; conversely, with the LO incident at port 4, ports 2 and 3 each take half and port 1 (RF) is isolated.The RF and LO never see each other, yet both can deliver signal to the two diodes — which is exactly the condition a mixer needs, “drive RF and LO into the diodes together without letting them interfere”. That is why the hybrid is the heart of a single-balanced mixer.

Common physical implementations (a recap)

  • Branch-line coupler: the $\lambda/4$ square ring in the figure above — the simplest, the most common, and the standard fare in Pozar.
  • Lange coupler: an interdigitated structure, broadband ($>1$ octave), widely used in MMICs.
  • Coupled-line directional coupler: two parallel transmission lines coupled together, broadside-coupled stripline.
  • Rat-race / magic-T: the 180° version; common in waveguide.
  • Lumped-element LC: for CMOS ICs below $<5$ GHz, as a substitute when a $\lambda/4$ line would be too large.

② The central block: a 3 dB 90° hybrid (branch-line implementation)

The “X” drawn inside the block on the original slide is merely an abstract schematic symbol(denoting four-port cross-coupling); this figure instead draws the most common physical implementation — branch-line coupler: a square ring made of four $\lambda/4$ microstrip lines.

  • The horizontal series arms (blue, thick): impedance $Z_0/\sqrt{2}\approx 35.4\,\Omega$ (in a 50 Ω system), length $\lambda/4$.
  • The vertical branch arms (red): impedance $Z_0=50\,\Omega$, length $\lambda/4$.
  • The four end points (ports ①②③④) are where the impedance transformation connects to the external 50 Ω system.

The length difference between the four $\lambda/4$ sections is the very reason the whole hybrid can produce a $-90°$ phase difference(every extra $\lambda/4$ travelled adds $-90°$).

The scattering matrix of the complete 4-port structure (port numbering as in ①②③④) is:

$$[S] = \frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & \cssId{rfd1a}{j} & \cssId{rfd2a}{1} & 0 \\ \cssId{rfd1b}{j} & 0 & 0 & \cssId{lod1a}{1} \\ \cssId{rfd2b}{1} & 0 & 0 & \cssId{lod2a}{j} \\ 0 & \cssId{lod1b}{1} & \cssId{lod2b}{j} & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
Reading the [S]-matrix elements (port 1 = RF in, 2 = diode 1, 3 = diode 2, 4 = LO in)

How to read it: $S_{ij}=$ the complex voltage ratio for a wave incident at port $j$ and emerging at port $i$. Every non-zero element has magnitude $1/\sqrt{2}$(an equal 3 dB split) plus an overall $-1$ prefix; the phase is set by $\{1, j\}$, with $1\to-180°$ and $j\to-90°$.

Element Value Path ($j\to i$) Physical meaning
$S_{11},S_{22},S_{33},S_{44}$ $0$ self → self All four ports are perfectly matched(return loss = ∞)
$S_{14}=S_{41}$ $0$ RF ↔ LO The RF and LO ports completely isolated

$S_{31}=S_{13}$ $\dfrac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}$ RF (1) → diode 2 (3) Coupled path: half power, $-180°$ phase shift

$S_{34}=S_{43}$ $\dfrac{-j}{\sqrt{2}}$ LO (4) → diode 2 (3) LO through to diode 2: half power, $-90°$

The crucial asymmetry: the RF goes 1→2 with $-90°$ and 1→3 with $-180°$; but the LO goes 4→2 with $-180°$ and 4→3 with $-90°$.The RF and LO phase differences of the two paths are exactly swapped, which is the root of writing $v_1$ and $v_2$ later as $\sin$ and $-\cos$, and the key to why the RF reflections cancel at port 1 while the LO reflections appear at port 4.

Symmetry: the matrix is symmetric($S_{ij}=S_{ji}$) → reciprocal; unitary($[S]^\dagger[S]=[I]$) → lossless; and the diagonal is all zeros → every port matched. Together these three properties pin it down as an ideal 4-port 90° hybrid.

③ The “$0°$” and “$-90°$” marked beside port 2 / port 3

These two angles are the RF signal's relative phase difference on arriving at port 2 and at port 3 respectively after entering at port 1 relative phase difference(taking the RF arrival at port 2 as the $0°$ reference):

  • RF (1) → diode 1 (port 2), the through path: passes through only one $\lambda/4$ series arm (the upper blue line) → total path length = $\lambda/4$ → absolute phase $-90°$. Taking port 2 as the reference, it is labelled $\mathbf{0°}$.
  • RF (1) → diode 2 (port 3), the coupled path: must pass through the left-hand $\lambda/4$ branch arm plus the lower $\lambda/4$ series arm → total path length = $\lambda/4 + \lambda/4 = \lambda/2$ → absolute phase $-180°$. Relative to port 2 it travels one extra $\lambda/4$, hence the label $\mathbf{-90°}$.

⚠ An easy point to misread: the “$-90°$” in the figure is port 3's phase relative to port 2 (one extra $\lambda/4$), not port 3's absolute phase. Port 3's absolute phase is in fact $-180°$ (a total path of $\lambda/2$), consistent with $S_{31}=-1/\sqrt{2}$ ($-180°$) in the $[S]$ matrix.

Note: the LO phase difference is exactly reversed
When the LO enters at port 4: LO → diode 1 is $-180°$ (the $1/\sqrt{2}$ term) and LO → diode 2 is $-90°$ (the $j/\sqrt{2}$ term).The RF and LO phase routes are exactly swapped, which is the crucial asymmetry of the 90° hybrid and the root of the $\sin$/$-\cos$ pairing that appears in $v_1, v_2$ later.

④ The two diodes (an anti-parallel pair)

Look closely at the figure: the two diodes point in opposite directions(the upper triangle points right, the lower one left). This is deliberately by design.

Why does the signal-flow diagram say “diode pair, ±a₁v²”?

① Where does $a_1 v^2$ come from? The I–V curve of a single diode is nonlinear; expanding the current as a Taylor series in the applied voltage:

$$i(v) = a_0 v + a_1 v^2 + a_2 v^3 + \cdots$$

⚠ The numbering convention in this course (and in Pozar) calls the second-order term$a_1$ (not $a_2$). So $a_1$ is the square-law coefficient, and it is the engine of the entire mixer — without the $v^2$ term there is no mixing:

$$v^2 = (V_{RF}\cos\omega_{RF}t + V_{LO}\cos\omega_{LO}t)^2 \;\supset\; V_{RF}V_{LO}\bigl[\cos(\omega_{RF}-\omega_{LO})t + \cos(\omega_{RF}+\omega_{LO})t\bigr]$$

That $\cos(\omega_{RF}-\omega_{LO})t$ is the IF we want. The linear term ($a_0 v$) merely passes the signal through and generates no new frequencies; higher-order terms from the cube upwards ($a_2 v^3$ and so on) produce IM3 and other distortion — but it is the square term that does the mixing.

② Where does the “$\pm$” come from? The two diodes are connected in series back to back(anti-parallel). Writing the upper one as $+a_1 v^2$, the lower one must carry a minus sign:

$$i_1(t) \approx \mathbf{+}a_1\bigl[v_1(t)+v_n(t)\bigr]^2, \qquad i_2(t) \approx \mathbf{-}a_1\bigl[v_2(t)+v_n(t)\bigr]^2$$

What is $v_n(t)$? It is the AM noise on the LO(amplitude noise) — a real LO is not a pure $V_{LO}\cos\omega_{LO}t$; its envelope jitters slightly with time:
$\quad v_{LO,\text{real}}(t) = [V_{LO} + v_n(t)]\cos\omega_{LO}t$
Because the hybrid is a linear passive network, this envelope perturbation is delivered equally to both diodes (common mode), so the same $v_n(t)$ appears in the small-signal model of each.It is precisely because it is common mode that the anti-parallel pair's $v_1^2 - v_2^2$ cancels it — that is the mathematical mechanism by which “a single-balanced mixer suppresses LO AM noise”. (PM noise is not in this model and must be handled separately.)

③ And then something magical happens when you sum them:

$$i_o = i_1 + i_2 \approx a_1\bigl[v_1^2 - v_2^2\bigr] + 2a_1 v_n(t)\bigl[v_1 - v_2\bigr]$$

Note that it is $v_1^2 \boldsymbol{-} v_2^2$ (a difference, not a sum!). Because the RF parts of $v_1$ and $v_2$ are in phase while the LO parts are in anti-phase (or vice versa, depending on the hybrid type), this Poor cancels the common-mode content (the LO second harmonic, the LO AM noise) and keeps only the RF×LO cross product — which is exactly the IF we want.

Step-by-step derivation: how is $i_o$ reduced from $i_1+i_2$?

Step 1 — expand each square. Using $(a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$:

$$\begin{aligned} i_1 &\approx a_1\bigl[v_1^2 + 2v_1 v_n + v_n^2\bigr] \\ i_2 &\approx -a_1\bigl[v_2^2 + 2v_2 v_n + v_n^2\bigr] \end{aligned}$$

Step 2 — add them.

$$i_o = i_1 + i_2 = a_1\bigl[v_1^2 + 2v_1 v_n + v_n^2\bigr] - a_1\bigl[v_2^2 + 2v_2 v_n + v_n^2\bigr]$$

Step 3 — distribute the $-a_1$ and group like terms:

$$i_o = a_1\bigl[v_1^2 - v_2^2\bigr] + 2a_1\bigl[v_1 v_n - v_2 v_n\bigr] + a_1\bigl[v_n^2 - v_n^2\bigr]$$

Step 4 — observe that $v_n^2$ cancels. The last bracket is exactly zero:

$$\underbrace{a_1 v_n^2}_{\text{from diode 1}} \;-\; \underbrace{a_1 v_n^2}_{\text{from diode 2}} = 0$$

This is the key highlight of the anti-parallel topology: because both diodes see the same $v_n(t)$ (common mode), the pure $v_n^2$ term (which, if it survived, would envelope-detect the LO AM noise straight into DC and the IF band) cancels exactly.

Step 5 — factor $v_n(t)$ out of the middle term:

$$2a_1\bigl[v_1 v_n - v_2 v_n\bigr] = 2a_1\,v_n(t)\bigl[v_1 - v_2\bigr]$$

The final result:

$$i_o(t) = i_1 + i_2 \;\approx\; a_1\bigl[v_1^2(t) - v_2^2(t)\bigr] \;+\; 2a_1\,v_n(t)\bigl[v_1(t) - v_2(t)\bigr]$$

The first bracket is the wanted mixing product(the IF emerges when it is expanded); the second bracket is the residual AM-noise term, but $v_1 - v_2$ contains only the RF and LO fundamentals (no DC, no IF), so multiplying by the slowly varying $v_n(t)$ puts everything near RF/LO, where it is filtered straight out by the following LPF.

Marked on the diodes:

  • $+v_1-$ / $+v_2-$: the instantaneous voltage polarity across each diode.
  • $i_1, i_2$: the current flowing out of each diode towards the common node.

⑤ The block on the right (summing junction)

Adding $i_1$ and $i_2$ at a common node gives

$$i_o(t) = i_1(t) + i_2(t) \approx a_1\bigl[v_1^2(t) - v_2^2(t)\bigr] + 2a_1 v_n(t)\bigl[v_1(t) - v_2(t)\bigr]$$

That $v_1^2-v_2^2$ (rather than $+$) is the handiwork of the anti-parallel diode pair.

⑥ Low-pass filter

Expanding the $i_o(t)$ above algebraically produces:

  • the difference frequency $\omega_{RF}-\omega_{LO}$ → wanted IF
  • the sum frequency $\omega_{RF}+\omega_{LO}$, plus $2\omega_{RF}$ and $2\omega_{LO}$ → unwanted
  • the AM-noise products $v_n(t)\cdot\sin\omega_{RF/LO}t$ → unwanted

The LPF removes all of the latter, leaving:

$$i_{IF}(t) \approx -a_1 V_{RF}V_{LO}\sin(\omega_{RF}-\omega_{LO})t = -\tfrac{1}{2}g'_{DC}\,V_{RF}V_{LO}\sin\omega_{IF}t$$

Where do $V_\Gamma^1, V_\Gamma^2$ come from? (a two-step round trip through the hybrid)

Step 1 — the hybrid sends the RF to the two diodes. The RF enters at port 1 and is distributed to ports 2 and 3 by the $[S]$ matrix:

$$V_1 = S_{21}\,V_{RF} = \frac{-j}{\sqrt{2}}V_{RF}, \qquad V_2 = S_{31}\,V_{RF} = \frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}V_{RF}$$

Step 2 — the mismatched diodes reflect $\Gamma$ times as much back. At RF the diode is a nonlinear load, not a pure 50 Ω; let its voltage reflection coefficient be $\Gamma$. The voltage launched back into the hybrid from the diode end is therefore:

$$V_\Gamma^1 = \Gamma\,V_1 = \frac{-j\Gamma V_{RF}}{\sqrt{2}}, \qquad V_\Gamma^2 = \Gamma\,V_2 = \frac{-\Gamma V_{RF}}{\sqrt{2}}$$

The “$\Gamma$” buried inside $V_\Gamma^1, V_\Gamma^2$ is the diode's own reflection coefficient; “$V_{RF}$” is the original input; and the leading $\dfrac{-j}{\sqrt{2}}$ and $\dfrac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}$ are the phase and amplitude of the hybrid's first split.

Step 3 — the hybrid redistributes these two reflections to port 1 (RF) and port 4 (LO). Because the hybrid is reciprocal ($S_{12}=S_{21}$), the return trip uses the same set of coefficients:

$$V_\Gamma^{RF} = S_{12}V_\Gamma^1 + S_{13}V_\Gamma^2, \qquad V_\Gamma^{LO} = S_{42}V_\Gamma^1 + S_{43}V_\Gamma^2$$

$S_{12}V_\Gamma^1$ = the signal reflected from diode 1, returning to the RF port along the hybrid's 2→1 path; $S_{13}V_\Gamma^2$ = the signal reflected from diode 2, returning to the RF port along the 3→1 path. Superposing the two gives the total reflection seen at the RF port.

The key result (the main point)
Substituting:
$$\begin{aligned} \color{#c01020}{\boldsymbol{V_\Gamma^{RF}}} &\color{#c01020}{\boldsymbol{= \frac{-j}{\sqrt{2}}\!\cdot\!\frac{-j\Gamma V_{RF}}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}\!\cdot\!\frac{-\Gamma V_{RF}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{-\Gamma V_{RF}}{2} + \frac{\Gamma V_{RF}}{2} = 0}} \quad\color{#c01020}{\boldsymbol{\text{(the reflection coefficient at the RF port is 0)}}} \\[4pt] V_\Gamma^{LO} &= \frac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}\!\cdot\!\frac{-j\Gamma V_{RF}}{\sqrt{2}} + \frac{-j}{\sqrt{2}}\!\cdot\!\frac{-\Gamma V_{RF}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{j\Gamma V_{RF}}{2} + \frac{j\Gamma V_{RF}}{2} = j\Gamma V_{RF} \end{aligned}$$
Even if the two diodes are completely mismatched, the RF port always looks perfectly matched ($V_\Gamma^{RF}=0$) — the two paths are exactly $180°$ apart and cancel. All of the reflection goes to the LO port instead (adding in phase), giving $|V_\Gamma^{LO}/V_{RF}|=|\Gamma|$. So what this 90° hybrid buys you is “the RF is always matched”, at the cost of “RF-to-LO isolation depends on how well the diodes are matched”.
Interpreting $j\Gamma V_{RF}$ and $|V_\Gamma^{LO}/V_{RF}|=|\Gamma|$

① $j\Gamma V_{RF}$ is the value of $V_\Gamma^{LO}$ — the reflected voltage that leaks to the LO port (port 4) when the diodes are mismatched. Broken down:

ComponentMeaning
$V_{RF}$the amplitude of the original RF input voltage
$\Gamma$the diode's voltage reflection coefficient at RF
$j$a $+90°$ phase factor (relative to the RF input)

So the leakage seen at the LO port has amplitude $= |\Gamma|\!\cdot\!|V_{RF}|$ and leads the RF input by $90°$ in phase. Where does the $j$ come from? When the two reflected paths travel through the hybrid to the LO port they happen to add in phase(unlike at the RF port, where they cancel), and their superposition leaves a residual $j$ phase factor.

② $|V_\Gamma^{LO}/V_{RF}|=|\Gamma|$ is the RF→LO coupling ratio (= the reciprocal of the RF-LO isolation).

Dividing both sides of $|V_\Gamma^{LO}|=|j\Gamma V_{RF}|=|\Gamma|\!\cdot\!|V_{RF}|$ by $|V_{RF}|$ gives:

$$\left|\frac{V_\Gamma^{LO}}{V_{RF}}\right| = |\Gamma|$$

Physical meaning: the fraction of the RF input that “leaks” to the LO port is exactly equal to the magnitude of the diode reflection coefficient.

Overall conclusion: the 90° hybrid version gives you two things — the RF port is unconditionally and perfectly matched($V_\Gamma^{RF}=0$) ✅; but the RF-LO isolation depends on how well you can match the diodes($|V_\Gamma^{LO}/V_{RF}|=|\Gamma|$) ⚠. That is the signature trade of this topology.

Signal-flow overview (the whole thing in one equation)

$$\underbrace{V_{RF}\cos\omega_{RF}t}_{\text{port 1}} + \underbrace{V_{LO}\cos\omega_{LO}t}_{\text{port 4}} \xrightarrow{\text{[S]}} \begin{cases} v_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[V_{RF}\sin\omega_{RF}t - V_{LO}\cos\omega_{LO}t] \\ v_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[-V_{RF}\cos\omega_{RF}t + V_{LO}\sin\omega_{LO}t] \end{cases} \xrightarrow{\substack{\text{diode pair}\\ \pm a_1 v^2}} i_o(t) \xrightarrow{\text{LPF}} i_{IF}(t)$$
Step-by-step derivation: the complete algebra of the three transitions from the LHS (RF + LO inputs) to the RHS ($i_{IF}(t)$)

Transition 1: $[S]$ matrix → $v_1, v_2$

The RF is incident at port 1 and the LO at port 4. Applying the hybrid's scattering matrix at ports 2 and 3:

$$V_2 = S_{21}V_{RF} + S_{24}V_{LO} = \tfrac{-j}{\sqrt{2}}V_{RF} + \tfrac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}V_{LO}$$ $$V_3 = S_{31}V_{RF} + S_{34}V_{LO} = \tfrac{-1}{\sqrt{2}}V_{RF} + \tfrac{-j}{\sqrt{2}}V_{LO}$$

Two rules for taking phasors back to the time domain:

  • multiplying by $-j = e^{-j\pi/2}$ ⇒ a time-domain phase of $-90°$: $\cos\omega t \to \sin\omega t$
  • multiplying by $-1 = e^{-j\pi}$ ⇒ a time-domain phase of $-180°$: $\cos\omega t \to -\cos\omega t$
Phasor coefficient Acting on Time-domain result
$-j/\sqrt{2}$$V_{RF}\cos\omega_{RF}t$$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}V_{RF}\sin\omega_{RF}t$
$-1/\sqrt{2}$$V_{LO}\cos\omega_{LO}t$$-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}V_{LO}\cos\omega_{LO}t$
$-1/\sqrt{2}$$V_{RF}\cos\omega_{RF}t$$-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}V_{RF}\cos\omega_{RF}t$
$-j/\sqrt{2}$$V_{LO}\cos\omega_{LO}t$$\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}V_{LO}\sin\omega_{LO}t$

Combining:

$$v_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl[V_{RF}\sin\omega_{RF}t - V_{LO}\cos\omega_{LO}t\bigr], \quad v_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl[-V_{RF}\cos\omega_{RF}t + V_{LO}\sin\omega_{LO}t\bigr]$$

Transition 2: the anti-parallel diode pair ($\pm a_1 v^2$) → $i_o(t)$

From section ④ above: $i_o = a_1[v_1^2 - v_2^2] + 2a_1 v_n(t)[v_1 - v_2]$. Substitute the time-domain $v_1, v_2$ and expand.

Expanding $v_1^2$:

$$v_1^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[V_{RF}^2\sin^2\omega_{RF}t - 2V_{RF}V_{LO}\sin\omega_{RF}t\cos\omega_{LO}t + V_{LO}^2\cos^2\omega_{LO}t\bigr]$$

Expanding $v_2^2$:

$$v_2^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[V_{RF}^2\cos^2\omega_{RF}t - 2V_{RF}V_{LO}\cos\omega_{RF}t\sin\omega_{LO}t + V_{LO}^2\sin^2\omega_{LO}t\bigr]$$

Subtracting and applying the trigonometric identities:

  • $\sin^2 x - \cos^2 x = -\cos 2x$
  • $\cos^2 x - \sin^2 x = +\cos 2x$
  • $\sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B = \sin(A-B)$
$$v_1^2 - v_2^2 = -\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl[V_{RF}^2\cos 2\omega_{RF}t + 2V_{RF}V_{LO}\sin(\omega_{RF}-\omega_{LO})t - V_{LO}^2\cos 2\omega_{LO}t\bigr]$$

$v_1 - v_2$ (needed for the AM-noise term):

$$v_1 - v_2 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl[V_{RF}(\sin\omega_{RF}t + \cos\omega_{RF}t) - V_{LO}(\cos\omega_{LO}t + \sin\omega_{LO}t)\bigr]$$

Combining gives the complete $i_o(t)$:

$$i_o(t) \approx -\tfrac{a_1}{2}\Bigl[V_{RF}^2\cos 2\omega_{RF}t + \underbrace{2V_{RF}V_{LO}\sin(\omega_{RF}-\omega_{LO})t}_{\text{wanted IF tone}} - V_{LO}^2\cos 2\omega_{LO}t\Bigr] + \sqrt{2}\,a_1 v_n(t)\bigl[V_{RF}(\sin+\cos)\omega_{RF}t - V_{LO}(\cos+\sin)\omega_{LO}t\bigr]$$

Transition 3: LPF → $i_{IF}(t)$

The low-pass filter is designed to pass $\omega_{IF}=\omega_{RF}-\omega_{LO}$ (typically MHz) and block everything near the RF/LO and their harmonics (GHz):

Frequency component Fate
$\cos 2\omega_{RF}t$ (≈ 2× RF)filtered out
$\cos 2\omega_{LO}t$ (≈ 2× LO)filtered out
$\sin(\omega_{RF}-\omega_{LO})t = \sin\omega_{IF}t$kept(this is the IF)
$v_n(t)\cdot\sin\omega_{RF/LO}t$ and similarfiltered out (the noise lands near RF/LO)

All that remains is the IF tone:

$$i_{IF}(t) \approx -\tfrac{a_1}{2}\cdot 2V_{RF}V_{LO}\sin(\omega_{RF}-\omega_{LO})t = -a_1 V_{RF}V_{LO}\sin\omega_{IF}t = -\tfrac{1}{2}g'_{DC}V_{RF}V_{LO}\sin\omega_{IF}t$$

The rightmost form is rewritten using the DC transconductance $g'_{DC}=2a_1$.

Three things in this result are worth noting:

  1. The amplitude is proportional to $V_{RF}\cdot V_{LO}$ — the signature of square-law mixing.
  2. There is no DC offset and no sum frequency $\omega_{RF}+\omega_{LO}$ — both were cancelled in $v_1^2-v_2^2$, leaving only the difference frequency.
  3. $v_n(t)$ has vanished completely from the IF band — AM noise can only modulate onto frequencies near RF/LO, and the LPF disposes of all of them. This is the mathematical origin of LO AM-noise suppression.

Three sentences to take away

  1. The ring inside the block is the branch-line coupler — two $Z_0/\sqrt{2}$ series arms (blue) plus two $Z_0$ branch arms (red), all $\lambda/4$; this is the real circuit behind the “X” schematic symbol on the original slide.
  2. The anti-parallel diode pair is the soul of this topology — it cancels the common-mode noise (including the LO AM noise) and keeps the differential-mode mixing product.
  3. The 90° hybrid kicks all the reflection out to the LO port — in exchange for the RF port always being perfectly matched. This is precisely the opposite of the 180° hybrid version (which kicks the reflection to the RF port to preserve LO isolation).
